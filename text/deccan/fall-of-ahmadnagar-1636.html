The end of Ahmadnagar

1636 · Treaty · Daulatabad, Maharashtra

Shah Jahan’s settlement of 1636 extinguishes the Nizam Shahi sultanate and binds Bijapur and Golconda to the Mughal empire as tributaries.

In 1636 Shah Jahan came to the Deccan in person, and by the end of the year the Ahmadnagar sultanate had ceased to exist. Daulatabad, the last Nizam Shahi citadel, had fallen to the Mughals in 1633 and the puppet sultan set up by Shahji Bhonsle had nowhere left to rule. The emperor divided the Nizam Shahi lands with Bijapur, which received roughly a third of them in the south, and took the rest into the Mughal subah.

The settlement came with two documents. Bijapur, under Muhammad Adil Shah, accepted a treaty that bound it to pay an indemnity, to abandon the Nizam Shahi cause, to cease interfering with Golconda and to expel Shahji from the Mughal frontier; in return it was confirmed in its new territory and left free to expand south. Golconda, under Abdullah Qutb Shah, signed what Mughal sources call a deed of submission, agreeing to an annual tribute, to strike coin and read the Friday sermon in Shah Jahan’s name and to drop the Safavid shah from both. Both states became, in the language of the day, protected kingdoms.

Shah Jahan then left the Deccan in the hands of his son Aurangzeb, viceroy from 1636 to 1644 and again from 1653 to 1658, with his seat at Malik Ambar’s old capital, renamed Aurangabad after him. For nearly fifty years the arrangement held, through repeated Mughal pressure for more tribute and a brief attack on Golconda in 1656.

The 1636 settlement was not a conquest, and the two sultanates used their freedom in the south to take over most of the Vijayanagara successor territory in Karnataka and the Coromandel plain. But it fixed the frame within which the rest of this period unfolds: a Mughal Deccan with two client states, and a Maratha military class released from Nizam Shahi service to look for new employers.

In the story

Tribute is the form of layered sovereignty that the Mughals preferred, and 1636 is its charter. Bijapur and Golconda kept their sultans, armies and revenues while acknowledging an emperor whose name was on their coins. The arrangement let them grow south at the expense of the nayakas even as they shrank in status, so that the Deccan was simultaneously becoming more Mughal and more sultanate-ruled. The collection treats the settlement as the moment the Deccan’s powers were ranked rather than merely counted.

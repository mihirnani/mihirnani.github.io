The Nagar revolt and the Mysore Commission

1830–1831 · Event · Nagara (Bidnur), Shivamogga district, Karnataka

A peasant rising in the Malnad gave the Company its reason to invoke the treaty of 1799 and take over the government of Mysore for fifty years.

In the second half of 1830 cultivators in the Nagar division of Mysore, the hilly country around the old Keladi capital of Bidnur, refused to pay their revenue and rose against the raja’s officers. The grievances were fiscal: a new system of revenue farming, the shurtee and sayar, had multiplied intermediaries and illegal collections, and the raja’s court, which had spent freely since Krishnaraja Wodeyar III took up the government in 1810, was pressing for money. The largely Lingayat peasantry also resented the Brahmin officers set over them. A pretender, Budi Basappa, who claimed the Keladi throne on the strength of forged documents and stolen seals, gave the rising a leader. The revolt spread through the Malnad in the winter of 1830 to 1831.

The raja’s troops could not suppress it, and Company forces from Bangalore were brought in. The fighting was over by June 1831 and the reprisals were severe; some ninety-nine rebels were executed at Honnali in March, and the total number killed is put between a hundred and sixty and seven hundred depending on the source. The Governor-General, Lord William Bentinck, treated the revolt as proof that the raja’s government had failed within the meaning of the 1799 treaty. On 19 October 1831 the raja was required to hand over the administration. Mysore was placed under a Commission, at first of several officers and from 1834 under Mark Cubbon alone, who governed it until 1861. Krishnaraja kept his title, his palace and an allowance.

A committee of inquiry later reported that the rising had been agrarian and not dynastic, and the raja spent decades petitioning for his state. The Company’s direct rule lasted until 1881, when Mysore was handed back to his adopted heir.

In the story

The Nagar revolt shows the reserved clause of the subsidiary system being used. In 1799 the Company had restored a raja and kept the right to remove his government; in 1831 it did so, without war, on the evidence of a tax rising. The sovereignty the Wodeyars had regained proved to be held on terms, and the terms were the Company’s to interpret. After Mysore, no Deccan ruler could regard his state as his own.

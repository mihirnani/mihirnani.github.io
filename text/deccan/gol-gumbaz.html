Gol Gumbaz

1656 · Place · Bijapur, Karnataka

The tomb of Muhammad Adil Shah at Bijapur, a single cube under one of the largest masonry domes ever built, left unfinished at his death in 1656.

The Gol Gumbaz is the mausoleum of Muhammad Adil Shah, sultan of Bijapur from 1627 to 1656, and of several of his household, including his wives, a daughter, a grandson and his favourite Rambha. Work began early in his reign, and seems to have stopped when he died in 1656; the exterior plaster and some ornament were never completed. The architect is not recorded by name.

The building is a cube about 47.5 metres on a side with a seven-storey octagonal turret at each corner, carrying a hemispherical dome whose external diameter is nearly 44 metres. The dome rests on eight intersecting pointed arches that gather the square into a circle, a solution without close parallel elsewhere in India, and it covers a single unbroken floor of some 1,700 square metres. A gallery runs round the inside of the drum where a whisper carries across the hall and a sound returns in multiple echoes, which has made the gallery as famous as the tomb. The cenotaphs stand on a platform in the middle; the burials are in a crypt below.

Muhammad Adil Shah’s reign was the last prosperous one in Bijapur’s history. The 1636 settlement with Shah Jahan made the sultanate a Mughal tributary but also freed it to expand south into the old Vijayanagara lands of Karnataka, and the revenues of that expansion paid for the tomb. His successor Ali Adil Shah II faced Shivaji on one side and Aurangzeb on the other, and the dynasty ended in 1686.

The Gol Gumbaz thus stands as a monument to a state at the moment before its decline, built at a scale the sultanate would never again be able to afford. It is the largest structure in a city that Vandermaelen’s 1827 map still labelled as the capital of a province, Bejapoor, a century and a half after it had ceased to be one.

In the story

A tomb is a statement about the future, and the Gol Gumbaz says that in the 1640s Bijapur expected to last. Thirty years later Aurangzeb camped beside it. The building belongs in this collection as the physical measure of what Mughal tribute did and did not take from a sultanate: the money and manpower for something this large remained in Bijapur’s hands even after 1636, and that was precisely why Aurangzeb came to take the rest.

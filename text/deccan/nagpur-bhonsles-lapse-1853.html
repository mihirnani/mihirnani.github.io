Nagpur, from Raghuji to the lapse

1743–1854 · Institution · Nagpur, Maharashtra

The fourth Maratha house, which ruled Berar and the Gond country from Nagpur for a century, raided Bengal, fought the Company at Sitabaldi, and ended in December 1853 as the largest of the states taken by lapse.

Raghuji Bhonsle, a kinsman of the Chhatrapati’s family whom Shahu made Senasahibsubha in 1728, took Nagpur from the Gond raja of Deogarh in the 1740s and made it the capital of a state that ran from Berar across the Gondwana forests to Orissa. His horsemen raided Bengal from 1742 until Alivardi Khan bought them off in 1751 with Orissa and the chauth of Bengal. The house was one of the four great sardar families of the confederacy, but the one that stayed a Deccan power, on the plateau’s north-eastern edge between Hyderabad and the Ganges plain. Raghuji died in 1755; his sons quarrelled; and Nagpur settled into the shape it kept for sixty years, a Maratha court ruling a country that was largely Gond, Telugu and Oriya, with Berar’s cotton revenue as its treasury and the Nizam and the Peshwa as its rivals.

The house met the Company’s armies twice. In 1803 Raghuji II marched with Scindia and shared the defeats at Assaye and Argaon; the Treaty of Deogaon in December took Cuttack and the country west of the Wardha from him, and the Nizam received the Berar districts. In November 1817 his nephew and successor Appa Sahib, secretly in league with the Peshwa, attacked the Residency; the two-day battle on the Sitabaldi hills, held by a small force of Madras sepoys, was the eastern front of the Third Maratha War. Appa Sahib was deposed the next year, and the Company put a boy, Raghuji III, on the gadi under a Resident, Richard Jenkins, who governed in his name until 1830 and then handed him a state whose army and foreign relations were already the Company’s.

Raghuji III died on 11 December 1853 without a son and without having adopted one. His widows proposed an adoption; Dalhousie declined to recognise one made after the raja’s death and declared the state lapsed to the paramount power, on the doctrine that had taken Satara five years earlier. Nagpur became a Company province in 1854 and, joined to the Saugor and Nerbudda territories, the Central Provinces in 1861; the raja’s jewels, horses and elephants were sold at auction, which was remembered. Of the four houses of the confederacy Nagpur was the only one to disappear. Holkar, Scindia and the Gaekwad survived 1858 as princes because they had heirs, treaties and, in 1857, the sense to stay loyal.

In the chronology

  • c. 1730s–1755Raghuji Bhonsle builds the Nagpur kingdom from Berar, expands into Gondwana and Odisha, and sponsors repeated Maratha invasions of Bengal from 1742 to 1751.
  • 1853–1854Raghoji III of Nagpur dies without a natural heir; Dalhousie annexes the Bhonsle state, bringing Nagpur and much of central India under direct Company rule.

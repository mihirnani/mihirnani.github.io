Amuktamalyada

c. 1510s–1520s · Document · Hampi, Karnataka

Krishnadevaraya’s Telugu court poem on the saint Andal contains a long passage of advice to kings, the nearest thing Vijayanagara left to a statement of how it was meant to be governed.

Amuktamalyada, ‘the giver of the worn garland’, is a long poem in Telugu attributed to Krishnadevaraya and composed during his reign, between 1509 and 1529; a precise date is not recorded. Its subject is Andal, the Tamil Vaishnava saint who offered the god garlands she had first worn herself, and her foster-father Vishnuchitta of Srivilliputtur. The prologue says that the god Vishnu appeared to the king in a dream and told him to write the story in Telugu. The poem is counted among the five great kavyas of Telugu literature, and its authorship by the king, though questioned by some, is accepted by most scholars.

What brings it into this collection is the fourth canto, in which Vishnuchitta’s discourse on kingship becomes a compact manual of statecraft. Forests in the heart of the kingdom should be cut back so that they do not shelter bandits. Ports should be kept in good order so that horses, elephants and precious stones can come in freely, and foreign merchants treated well. Forts should be put in the charge of Brahmins, who are loyal and literate, rather than of kinsmen who may rebel. Even a king with little land should build wells and tanks. Taxes should be taken gently. The subordinate lords are to be kept busy and watched.

The passage is practical, in places blunt, and it corresponds to what the king actually did: the Portuguese horse trade, the Brahmin fort commanders noticed by Nunes, the irrigation works around the capital. Srinivas Reddy’s translation, Giver of the Worn Garland (2010), made it accessible in English.

Vijayanagara produced no Persian-style chronicle of its own, so the poem, with the Portuguese accounts, carries much of the weight of explaining its political ideas. It shows a kingship concerned with revenue, trade and control of its own nobility, familiar ground to any sultan across the river.

In the story

The collection uses documents to show how rulers described their own sovereignty. Amuktamalyada is Vijayanagara’s contribution, and what it says is strikingly unsectarian: keep the ports open, manage the chiefs, garrison the forts with people who will not rebel. It reads as the advice of a ruler who knew his authority was layered over nayakas and merchants and could be lost to them. Placed beside the later Ajnapatra of the Marathas, it shows a continuous Deccan idiom of statecraft across three centuries.

Chauth and sardeshmukhi

c. 1665–1818 · Institution · Satara, Maharashtra

The quarter and the tenth: the Maratha claim to a fixed share of other sovereigns’ revenue, first levied by Shivaji, chartered by the emperor in 1719, and the legal form that shared sovereignty took in the eighteenth-century Deccan.

Chauth was a quarter. In the western Deccan of the seventeenth century the word already meant a share of the land revenue paid to a power that could otherwise ravage a district – the Portuguese of Daman paid it to the Koli chiefs of Ramnagar for the peace of their northern villages – and Shivaji made it a claim of state. From the 1660s his officers demanded a fourth of the assessed revenue of Mughal and Bijapuri districts that he did not govern, in Khandesh and the Surat country among others, in return for leaving them alone. The payment did not transfer the district, only a fraction of its income: a Mughal faujdar and a Maratha collector might work the same villages in the same season. Sardeshmukhi was a further tenth, claimed on a different ground. The sardeshmukh was the chief of the hereditary district headmen, and Shivaji asserted the office over the whole of his swarajya as a right older than any sultan or emperor, which none of them had conferred and none could withdraw.

The two claims paid for the long war after 1689. Rajaram at Jinji and Tarabai after him could not maintain an army from a territory they did not hold, so they granted their commanders the right to collect chauth and sardeshmukhi from Mughal districts, as saranjam, and the commanders collected what they could; the Maratha polity that emerged after 1707 was in large part a federation of such grants. The sanads of 1719 then gave the arrangement an imperial seal. The emperor granted Shahu the chauth and sardeshmukhi of the six Mughal subahs of the Deccan – Khandesh, Berar, Aurangabad, Bidar, Bijapur and Hyderabad – and the Marathas undertook to keep the peace they were being paid for. Under the Peshwa’s rules the chauth was itself divided: a quarter of it, the babti, went to the Chhatrapati; a small share, the sahotra, to the Pant Sachiv; and the bulk, the mokasa, to the commanders who collected it and kept horse on it. Sardeshmukhi went to the king.

What this meant for sovereignty is the subject of André Wink’s Land and Sovereignty in India. On his reading the Maratha claim was not a tribute exacted from a defeated empire but a share in it – a co-sovereignty negotiated district by district, in which the Mughal governor kept his office and the Maratha kamavisdar kept his fourth, and each recognised the other as the condition of collecting anything at all. The Nizam’s Hyderabad after 1724 lived under exactly this partition, enforced at Palkhed and contested to Kharda; so did Malwa and Gujarat once the Peshwa carried the claim north of the Narmada. The Company inherited the claim in 1818 with the Peshwa’s other rights and let it lapse, because a paramount power collects revenue and does not share it.

In the story

Chauth is the mechanism behind the collection’s argument: the fraction that let two states hold one territory and one taxpayer with a legal title each. Most of the entries from Palkhed to the Peshwa’s surrender are, underneath, about who held it.

In the chronology

  • 1660s–1670sShivaji levies chauth, a quarter of the assessed revenue, on Mughal and Bijapuri districts he does not govern, and claims the sardeshmukhi over his swarajya; the two claims become the legal form of Maratha co-sovereignty.

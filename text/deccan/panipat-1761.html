Panipat, 1761

14 January 1761 · Battle · Panipat, Haryana

The Maratha defeat by Ahmad Shah Durrani north of Delhi killed a generation of commanders and ended the Peshwa’s centralised bid to dominate the Mughal empire.

On 14 January 1761, on the plain of Panipat north of Delhi, the Afghan ruler Ahmad Shah Durrani (Abdali), with the Rohilla chiefs and Shuja-ud-Daula of Awadh, destroyed the Maratha army commanded by Sadashivrao Bhau, the Peshwa’s cousin. The Marathas had come north in 1760 to restore their hold on the Mughal court after Durrani’s invasions, had taken Delhi and Kunjpura, and had then been cut off from their supplies and starved through the autumn. They attacked rather than starve. The Peshwa’s son Vishwasrao was killed in the afternoon, Bhau died fighting, and the army broke. Contemporary estimates of the dead ran to sixty or seventy thousand, including thousands of prisoners and camp followers killed the next day. The artillery commander Ibrahim Khan Gardi, a former French-trained officer of the Nizam, was captured and executed.

The defeat had been prepared in the Deccan. A year earlier, on 3 February 1760 at Udgir, Bhau had beaten the Nizam’s army and taken Ahmadnagar, Daulatabad, Burhanpur and Bijapur, the best of the old sultanate cities; the northern expedition was launched from that success with the Deccan apparently secure. No Rajput or Jat ruler joined the Marathas at Panipat; the chauth had made them few friends. Malhar Rao Holkar left the field, and was blamed for it at Pune. The Peshwa Balaji Bajirao, who had set out too late with reinforcements, turned back on hearing the news and died at Pune on 23 June 1761.

For the Deccan the consequences were immediate. Nizam Ali Khan marched on Pune within the year and had to be bought off. Haidar Ali, whose takeover of Mysore was completed in 1761, faced no Maratha interference until 1764. The sixteen-year-old Peshwa Madhavrao inherited a treasury emptied, an army gone, and an uncle, Raghunathrao, who wanted his office. Maratha power recovered under Madhavrao and re-entered Delhi in 1771, but it recovered as a set of houses, each with its own army and interest, rather than as the single instrument Bajirao had made; and the Company, which had taken Bengal in 1757 and Pondicherry in 1761, was now the one power in India whose strength was growing.

In the story

Panipat closes the Maratha century because it settled who would not be paramount. The Peshwa’s state had come nearer than any Indian power to replacing the Mughals as a single sovereign for the subcontinent, and on one day lost the capacity to try. In the Deccan the result was a return to the four-cornered balance of Pune, Hyderabad, Mysore and the Company, and the Company was the only corner still gaining.

The Maratha navy

1664–1667 · Place · Sindhudurg, Malvan, Maharashtra

Shivaji’s sea fort at Malvan, begun in November 1664 with the Surat plunder, and the fleet he built to face the Siddis of Janjira and the Europeans.

The foundation stone of Sindhudurg was laid on 25 November 1664 on Kurte, a rocky island off Malvan in the southern Konkan, and the fort was completed in about 1667. Its building was supervised by Hiroji Indulkar, with help from Portuguese engineers from Goa; the rampart runs for nearly three kilometres around the island, and the money came from the Surat raid of that January. It was the largest of a chain of coastal forts, with Vijaydurg, Suvarnadurg and Kolaba, that gave the Marathas bases along the Konkan.

Shivaji had begun building ships around 1657, when he took the Kalyan and Bhiwandi creeks from Bijapur, and by the 1670s the fleet is reported at several hundred vessels of various sizes, mostly small and oared, crewed by Koli and Bhandari fishermen and commanded at first by Muslim officers, Daulat Khan and Darya Sarang. Its purpose was defensive and fiscal: to protect the coastal districts, to collect customs, and to deal with the Siddis of Janjira.

The Siddis, Habshi naval commanders of Ahmadnagar and then Bijapur, held the island fort of Janjira near Murud and in 1670 transferred their allegiance to the Mughals, who used them as the imperial fleet on the west coast. Shivaji’s repeated attempts to take Janjira by siege and by assault, continued by Sambhaji in the 1680s, all failed, and the Siddi state survived until 1948. The Maratha fleet could hold the coast but could not take a fortified island.

After the wars of the 1690s the fleet passed to Kanhoji Angre, whose family made it a power the Company and the Portuguese had to reckon with until 1756. Sindhudurg itself became the seat of the Bhonsles of Sawantwadi and Kolhapur’s admirals, and contains a small temple to Shivaji, the only one of its kind.

In the story

The Konkan coast in the 1660s had more sovereigns per mile than anywhere in India: Portuguese at Goa and Bassein, Siddis at Janjira, the Company at Bombay, Bijapur’s officers in the ports and now a Maratha fleet. Sindhudurg is the collection’s way into that crowded sea. It shows the Maratha state trying to act as a maritime power, succeeding against merchants and failing against an island fort, a limit that the Company would exploit when it destroyed the Angre fleet a century later.

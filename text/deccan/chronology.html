Chronology

The Deccan, year by year

Every dated line in the collection, including context events that have no entry of their own; where the collection has an entry for a line, it is linked at the end of it.

Backdrop – Delhi comes south 1296–1336

  • 1296Alauddin Khalji raids Devagiri, the Yadava capital; tribute is imposed.
  • 1307/08–1311Malik Kafur’s campaigns: Devagiri is reduced (1307/08), Warangal attacked (1309–10), then Dwarasamudra and the Tamil country invaded (1310–11).
  • 1317–1318Delhi reoccupies Devagiri; Harapaladeva is defeated and executed, and the Yadava state is extinguished.
  • 1323Ulugh Khan, the future Muhammad bin Tughluq, takes Warangal; the Kakatiya kingdom ends.
  • 1327Muhammad bin Tughluq makes Devagiri – renamed Daulatabad – a second imperial capital and transfers much of the Delhi court and population south. Entry →
  • c. 1327–1336Kampili falls; traditions later connect refugees and former officers from this frontier world with the rise of Vijayanagara, though the details of Harihara and Bukka’s early careers remain disputed.
  • c. 1335Madurai breaks away from Delhi as an independent sultanate. Entry →

Two Foundations c. 1330–1500

  • c. 1325–1475Reddi and Velama polities rule parts of coastal Andhra and Telangana between larger powers. Entry →
  • c. 1336–1346Vijayanagara takes shape on the Tungabhadra beside the Pampa-Virupaksha sacred complex at Hampi; 1336 is the conventional foundation date, while state formation was a process extending across the following decade. Entry →
  • 1342Hoysala Ballala III is captured and killed while campaigning against the Madurai sultanate; Hoysala territories are progressively absorbed into Vijayanagara. Entry →
  • 3 August 1347Zafar Khan is proclaimed Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah; contemporary and later sources differ on the precise setting of the proclamation, but Gulbarga becomes the first Bahmani capital. Entry →
  • 14th–15th c.The west-coast war-horse trade, temple-centred landed orders and nayankara military service in the south, and the contested Raichur doab become durable structures of Deccan politics. Entry →
  • 1360s–1420sRepeated Bahmani–Vijayanagara wars establish the Krishna- Tungabhadra frontier and the Raichur doab as a long-running theatre rather than a permanently held border. Entry →
  • c. 1352–1371Kumara Kampana’s southern campaigns reduce the Sambuvarayas and defeat the Madurai sultanate; Vijayanagara power reaches the far south. Entry →
  • c. 1382–1601The Faruqi dynasty establishes an effectively independent Khandesh state on the Tapti frontier of the Deccan; Burhanpur and Asirgarh become major political and commercial centres.
  • c. 1396–1407The ‘Durga Devi famine’ of later Deccan tradition is remembered as a prolonged subsistence crisis; its chronology, duration and geographical extent are uncertain.
  • 1398Timur sacks Delhi; the northern sultanate that had once sought to govern the Deccan is no longer a sustained southern power.
  • r. 1397–1422Firuz Shah Bahmani rules; the Chishti saint Muhammad Gisu Daraz (Gesudaraz) settles at Gulbarga after 1400. Entry →
  • r. 1422–1436Ahmad Shah I Bahmani shifts the political centre toward Bidar and campaigns aggressively in Telangana; Warangal is defeated in the mid-1420s, bringing much – but not all – of Telangana under Bahmani power.
  • r. 1424–1446Deva Raya II presides over Vijayanagara’s fifteenth-century high point. Entry →
  • c. 1425–1432The Bahmani capital moves from Gulbarga to Bidar. Entry →
  • 1430s–1460sThe Gajapati rulers of Odisha expand into coastal Andhra and periodically threaten Vijayanagara and Bahmani interests, making the eastern Deccan a major three-sided frontier. Entry →
  • 1442–1444Abdur Razzaq’s embassy to Vijayanagara leaves one of the best-known fifteenth-century descriptions of the capital. Entry →
  • 1446–1465Mallikarjuna’s reign marks Sangama decline amid Gajapati and Bahmani pressure.
  • 1450s–1481Mahmud Gawan becomes the dominant Bahmani minister; provincial and fiscal reforms strengthen the centre, his madrasa is built at Bidar in the 1470s, Goa is taken for the Bahmanis in 1472, and he is executed in 1481. Entry →
  • c. 1469–1472Afanasy Nikitin travels through the Deccan and leaves a rare Russian account of Bahmani India. Entry →
  • 1480s–1528Bahmani authority fragments; provincial governors and military houses become increasingly autonomous, while Barid ministers dominate the last Bahmani sultans at Bidar. The final claimant leaves Bidar in 1528. Entry →
  • 1485Saluva Narasimha displaces the Sangama dynasty at Vijayanagara. Entry →
  • 20 May 1498Vasco da Gama reaches Calicut, opening a new phase of Portuguese intervention in Indian Ocean commerce. Entry →
  • 1505Vira Narasimha establishes the Tuluva dynasty at Vijayanagara.

Two Deccans 1500–1565

  • c. 1490–1518Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Berar, Golconda and Bidar emerge from the Bahmani break-up; independence was staggered and the exact founding dates differ by dynasty. Entry →
  • 1490–1528Fathullah Imad-ul-Mulk rules Berar from Elichpur; the Barids govern Bidar behind the last Bahmanis until Kalimullah leaves in 1528. Entry →
  • c. 1500Vijayanagara at its height, among the largest cities in the world. Entry →
  • 1500–1509Cabral and da Gama’s second voyage, Almeida’s viceroyalty, and the Portuguese victory at Diu (1509) create a powerful – though never complete – Portuguese naval predominance in the western Indian Ocean.
  • 25 November 1510Afonso de Albuquerque takes Goa. Portuguese control gives the Estado da Índia major leverage over the Arabian and Persian horse trade to western India, but never an absolute monopoly. Entry →
  • 1509–1529Krishnadevaraya’s reign marks Vijayanagara’s political and monumental zenith; campaigns against the Gajapatis between 1512 and 1519 establish Vijayanagara predominance south of the Krishna in the east, and the Telugu poem Amuktamalyada is associated with his court. Entry →
  • 19 May 1520At Raichur, Krishnadevaraya defeats Ismail Adil Shah and takes the fort, the best-documented set-piece battle of the Vijayanagara- sultanate wars. Entry →
  • c. 1520–1522Domingo Paes describes Vijayanagara; Fernao Nuniz’s chronicle follows in the 1530s. Entry →
  • 1522 / 1537Shah Tahir arrives at Ahmadnagar in 1522; in 1537 Burhan Nizam Shah I proclaims Twelver Shi’ism the official religion of the Nizam Shahi state. Entry →
  • 23 December 1534Bahadur Shah of Gujarat cedes Bassein and associated rights to Portugal; the Portuguese Northern Province becomes a major coastal power adjoining the Deccan until the Maratha conquest of 1739. Entry →
  • 1518–1590The early Qutb Shahis consolidate Golconda; diamonds from the Krishna valley and expanding east-coast trade become major sources of wealth. Entry →
  • 27 July 1550Ibrahim Qutb Shah succeeds at Golconda after years in Vijayanagara exile; his reign deepens Telugu-Persianate cultural synthesis. Hussain Sagar is constructed in 1562. Entry →
  • 1529–1542Achyutadevaraya rules Vijayanagara; his death is followed by a succession crisis.
  • 1542–1565Sadashiva Raya remains the crowned sovereign while Aliya Rama Raya becomes the effective ruler and intervenes repeatedly in sultanate politics. Entry →
  • 1550s–1560sBijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda and Bidar repeatedly change alliances with one another and with Vijayanagara; the coalition of 1565 was therefore the outcome of fluid interstate politics, not a permanent religious bloc.
  • 1560The Goa Inquisition is established; the First Provincial Council follows in 1567. Entry →
  • 23 January 1565At Rakshasi-Tangadi, commonly called Talikota, the allied sultanate army defeats Vijayanagara and Rama Raya is killed. The metropolitan complex at Vijayanagara is plundered and substantially abandoned over the following months, but the empire survives under the Aravidu dynasty. Entry →
  • 1565–mid-17th c.Aravidu Vijayanagara continues from Penukonda, Chandragiri and later Vellore; imperial authority fragments among regional nayakas and successor houses through the seventeenth century. Entry →

The Mughal South 1565–1687

  • 1574 / 1619Ahmadnagar annexes Berar in 1574; Bijapur incorporates Bidar in 1619. Entry →
  • c. 1570–1665Masulipatnam rises from a regional textile outlet to a major Bay of Bengal and western Indian Ocean port under Qutb Shahi patronage; Persian, Armenian, Indian and later Dutch and English merchants connect the eastern Deccan to Southeast Asia and the Red Sea.
  • r. 1580–1627Ibrahim Adil Shah II rules Bijapur; the Kitab-i-Nauras and court patronage make his reign a high point of Dakhni literary and musical culture. Entry →
  • 1591Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah founds Hyderabad beside the Musi, shifting the Qutb Shahi political centre beyond Golconda fort. Entry →
  • 1595–1600Chand Bibi leads the defence of Ahmadnagar during Mughal intervention in the succession crisis; Akbar’s forces take Ahmadnagar city in 1600, but the Nizam Shahi state survives. Entry →
  • August 1600Prince Daniyal and the Khan-i-Khanan storm Ahmadnagar fort after a four-month siege; Akbar organises Khandesh, Berar and Ahmadnagar as the first Mughal subahs south of the Narmada, and takes Asirgarh in January 1601. Entry →
  • 1601Akbar conquers Khandesh after the fall of Asirgarh, ending the Faruqi dynasty and carrying Mughal administration to the Deccan’s northern frontier.
  • c. 1548–1626Malik Ambar rebuilds Nizam Shahi power through mobile warfare, revenue reform and alliance; Khirki, later Aurangabad, is developed as his capital, and Mughal forces are defeated at Bhatvadi in 1624. Entry →
  • c. 1608–1649Tukaram at Dehu becomes the best-known poet-saint of the seventeenth-century Varkari flowering; Ramdas (1608–1681) represents another influential devotional current. Entry →
  • 1610Raja Wodeyar takes control of Srirangapatna, the former Vijayanagara viceregal seat, establishing the capital from which the Mysore state expands over the next century and a half. Entry →
  • 1611–1668The English East India Company establishes factories at Masulipatnam, Madras and eventually Bombay – footholds on the Deccan’s maritime approaches rather than a single territorial advance. Entry →
  • 1594–1664Shahji Bhonsle serves successively and sometimes simultaneously within Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Mughal political worlds; his career exemplifies mobile service elites before Shivaji’s independent power. Entry →
  • 1630–1632A severe famine strikes Gujarat and the Deccan; Mughal pressure continues, Daulatabad falls in 1633, and the Nizam Shahi kingdom is extinguished in 1636. Entry →
  • 1636Shah Jahan’s Deccan settlement partitions former Ahmadnagar territory and compels Bijapur and Golconda to accept Mughal suzerainty and defined frontiers; the settlement gives both sultanates room for major southward expansion. Entry →
  • 1646Shivaji takes Torna and begins the sustained acquisition of forts and local authority in the western Deccan. Entry →
  • 1656–1657Bijapur’s succession crisis invites Mughal intervention; Aurangzeb campaigns in the Deccan before the imperial war of succession calls him north. The Gol Gumbaz mausoleum complex belongs to the same mid-century Bijapur high point. Entry →
  • 10 November 1659Afzal Khan is killed during his meeting with Shivaji below Pratapgad; the ensuing Maratha victory transforms the balance in the western Deccan. Entry →
  • 1660Siddi Jauhar besieges Panhala; Shivaji escapes toward Vishalgad, followed by the rear-guard action remembered at Pavan Khind.
  • 1663–1664Shivaji raids Shaista Khan’s Pune quarters, sacks Surat, and begins construction of Sindhudurg. Entry →
  • 1665–1666The Treaty of Purandar is followed by Shivaji’s journey to Agra, confinement and escape. Entry →
  • 1660s–1670sShivaji levies chauth, a quarter of the assessed revenue, on Mughal and Bijapuri districts he does not govern, and claims the sardeshmukhi over his swarajya; the two claims become the legal form of Maratha co-sovereignty. Entry →
  • 1670–1672Maratha forces recover key forts, sack Surat again and win at Salher, demonstrating the capacity to defeat a Mughal army in a large open battle.
  • 6 June 1674Shivaji is crowned at Raigad in a Sanskritic kingship ceremony that asserts autonomous royal status. The conventional Ashtapradhan council belongs to the institutional vocabulary of his court, though its practical operation varied. Entry →
  • c. 1674–1686Madanna and Akkanna dominate Golconda’s administration; Shivaji negotiates at Hyderabad and campaigns in the Karnataka in 1677. Entry →
  • 3 April 1680Shivaji dies at Raigad; Sambhaji secures the succession after a court struggle.
  • 1681The rebellion of Aurangzeb’s son Akbar and his flight toward the Marathas helps bring the emperor permanently to the Deccan.
  • 1686–September 1687Bijapur surrenders to Aurangzeb in 1686; Golconda falls after a long siege in September 1687, when Mughal troops enter following the opening of a gate from within. Entry →
  • 1687–1707The Mughals attempt to assimilate the former Bijapur and Golconda kingdoms into the imperial provincial system, creating an enormous Deccan command that proves costly to govern and defend.
  • 1680–1689Sambhaji fights the Mughals, Siddis and Portuguese; captured at Sangameshwar in early 1689, he is executed on 11 March. Entry →

The Maratha Century 1687–1761

  • 1689–1698Rajaram escapes to Jinji; the long siege there allows a dispersed Maratha command structure to continue resistance across the Deccan. Entry →
  • c. 1695–1710Papadu builds a short-lived armed domain in Mughal Telangana and sacks Warangal in 1708. Entry →
  • 1699–1705Aurangzeb’s final fort campaign repeatedly captures Maratha strongholds only for many to be lost again; the war becomes a drain on Mughal money, manpower and command.
  • 1700–1707Rajaram dies in 1700; Tarabai governs in the name of her young son and sustains the anti-Mughal war. Entry →
  • 3 March 1707Aurangzeb dies near Ahmadnagar. Shahu is released from Mughal captivity; his victory over Tarabai’s forces at Khed begins a divided Maratha kingship. Entry →
  • 1708–1731Satara and Kolhapur emerge as rival Chhatrapati courts; the Treaty of Warna in 1731 formalises the division. Entry →
  • 1713–1720Balaji Vishwanath becomes Peshwa and turns the office into the principal executive instrument of Shahu’s expanding state; negotiations at Delhi culminate in the imperial grants of 1719.
  • 19 November 1715The Ajnapatra, traditionally attributed to Ramchandra Pant Amatya but with disputed authorship, sets out a Marathi language of kingship, forts, service, revenue and political prudence. Entry →
  • 1713–1729Kanhoji Angre dominates much of the Konkan naval and coastal system, compelling Europeans and Maratha rivals alike to negotiate with his house. Entry →
  • 1719Mughal sanads recognise Shahu’s claims to chauth and sardeshmukhi from the six Deccan subahs, giving an imperial documentary form to Maratha fiscal claims. Entry →
  • 11 October 1724At Shakar Kheda, Nizam-ul-Mulk defeats Mubariz Khan and establishes the durable autonomy of the Asaf Jahi state while retaining Mughal titles and political idioms. Entry →
  • 28 February 1728Bajirao I outmanoeuvres the Nizam in the Palkhed campaign and forces a settlement favourable to Shahu. Entry →
  • 1720–1740As Peshwa, Bajirao I drives Maratha power north into Malwa and Gujarat, raids toward Delhi in 1737 and forces the Nizam to terms after the Bhopal campaign. Entry →
  • February–May 1739Chimaji Appa’s campaign captures Bassein and most of Portugal’s Northern Province, ending the Portuguese position as a major territorial power north of Goa. Entry →
  • 1730s–1761Holkar, Scindia, Gaekwad and Bhonsle houses acquire regional domains while acknowledging varying degrees of Chhatrapati and Peshwa authority; the Maratha polity becomes a confederated, negotiated imperial system. Entry →
  • c. 1730s–1755Raghuji Bhonsle builds the Nagpur kingdom from Berar, expands into Gondwana and Odisha, and sponsors repeated Maratha invasions of Bengal from 1742 to 1751. Entry →
  • 1746–1763The Carnatic wars entwine Hyderabad’s succession disputes, the Arcot nawabship and Anglo-French rivalry; the 1751 defence of Arcot is a major Company breakthrough. Entry →
  • 1748–1762After Nizam-ul-Mulk’s death, Nasir Jang, Muzaffar Jang, Salabat Jang and Nizam Ali compete for the Hyderabad succession; French and British military support becomes an important resource within Deccani politics rather than an external sideshow.
  • 1751–1758Bussy and French-trained troops become central to Salabat Jang’s regime; the Northern Circars are assigned to the French in 1753 before passing progressively into Company control in the 1760s. Entry →
  • February 1756At Vijaydurg, Company and Peshwa forces destroy Tulaji Angre’s power; the Angre naval system ceases to operate as an autonomous regional force.
  • 2–3 February 1760At Udgir, Maratha forces defeat Nizam Ali and win extensive territorial concessions from Hyderabad. Entry →
  • 14 January 1761The Maratha army is defeated at Panipat by Ahmad Shah Durrani and his allies. In the same period Haidar Ali becomes the dominant military and political figure in Mysore. Entry →

Mysore and the Company Wars 1761–1799

  • 1761–1772Peshwa Madhavrao I restores Maratha finances and military authority after Panipat; the victory at Rakshasbhuvan in August 1763 checks Nizam Ali. Entry →
  • 1762–1763Nizam Ali displaces Salabat Jang and shifts the Asaf Jahi political centre decisively from Aurangabad to Hyderabad. Entry →
  • c. 1730–1803Banking houses and military financiers become indispensable to the states of Hyderabad, Pune and Bombay; public revenue, private credit and military mobilisation form a single political economy. Entry →
  • 1760s–1790sHyderabad develops a distinct post-Mughal political system centred on hereditary record-keepers, revenue contractors, nobles, jagirdars, military entrepreneurs and banking firms linked by patronage and vakils.
  • 1767–1769The First Anglo-Mysore War ends with the Treaty of Madras on terms favourable to Haidar Ali. Entry →
  • 30 August 1773Peshwa Narayanrao is murdered in the Shaniwar Wada; Raghunathrao claims power and the Barbhai council forms around the posthumous heir Madhavrao II. Entry →
  • 1775The Treaty of Surat between Raghunathrao and the Bombay government opens direct Company intervention in the Peshwa succession and the First Anglo-Maratha War.
  • 1774–1800Nana Phadnavis becomes the central civilian manager of the Pune government during Madhavrao II’s minority and the confederacy’s prolonged factional politics. Entry →
  • 1779–1782Company forces capitulate at Wadgaon in 1779; the wider First Anglo-Maratha War ends with the Treaty of Salbai in 1782, restoring a negotiated balance between Pune and the Company. Entry →
  • 10 September 1780At Pollilur, Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan destroy Colonel Baillie’s Company detachment during the Second Anglo-Mysore War. Entry →
  • 1782–1784Haidar Ali dies; Tipu Sultan succeeds him, and the Treaty of Mangalore restores the pre-war position after the Second Anglo-Mysore War. Entry →
  • 1784–1799Tipu reorganises revenue and military institutions, develops state monopolies and workshops, expands rocket forces and sends embassies to the Ottoman, French and other courts. Entry →
  • 1790–1792The Third Anglo-Mysore War pits Tipu against a triple alliance of the Company, Hyderabad and the Marathas; the allied army reaches Srirangapatna in 1792.
  • April 1791Irregulars attached to the Maratha army sack the Sringeri matha; Tipu Sultan’s letters ordering its restoration, found in 1916, are the best-documented act of his religious policy. Entry →
  • 1791–1792The Doji Bara or ‘skull famine’ devastates wide areas of the Deccan after repeated monsoon failures, interacting with war and grain-market disruption. Entry →
  • 18 March 1792The Treaty of Seringapatam forces Tipu to cede roughly half his dominions to the allied powers and deliver hostages pending payment of the indemnity. Entry →
  • 12 February 1794Mahadji Scindia dies at Wanavdi near Pune after making his house the dominant Maratha power in north India. Entry →
  • 11 March 1795At Kharda, the combined Maratha leadership defeats the Nizam in the confederacy’s last major joint victory over Hyderabad. Entry →
  • 1795–1796Madhavrao II dies; Bajirao II becomes Peshwa and factional conflict intensifies at Pune.
  • 1798Under Lord Wellesley’s system, Hyderabad accepts an expanded subsidiary alliance and dismisses the French-trained corps, sharply increasing Company control over the state’s external and military affairs. Entry →
  • 4 May 1799Srirangapatna is stormed in the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War and Tipu Sultan is killed; the Company partitions much of Mysore and restores a Wodeyar dynasty under subsidiary control. Entry →

Paramountcy 1799–1831

  • 30 June 1799The Wodeyar restoration is formally installed at Mysore; Colin Mackenzie’s surveys and collections expand in the post-war south. Entry →
  • 1799–1800Dhondia Wagh builds a mobile armed following across the Mysore-Maratha frontier before being hunted down by Company forces.
  • 1800The Nizam cedes the ‘Ceded Districts’ of Rayalaseema to maintain the subsidiary force, deepening the territorial consequences of the alliance. Entry →
  • 10 April 1802William Lambton measures his first baseline near Madras; the survey that becomes the Great Trigonometrical Survey begins. Entry →
  • 25 October 1802Yashwantrao Holkar defeats the Peshwa and Scindia outside Pune; Bajirao II flees to Company protection. Entry →
  • 31 December 1802The Treaty of Bassein restores Bajirao II in return for a subsidiary alliance and restrictions on independent diplomacy, helping precipitate the Second Anglo-Maratha War. Entry →
  • 1803Company armies defeat Scindia and Raghuji II Bhonsle in two major theatres: Assaye (23 September), Delhi and Laswari in the north, and Argaon (29 November) and Gawilghur in the Deccan. Entry →
  • 1804–1805Yashwantrao Holkar’s separate war continues after Scindia and the Bhonsle have made peace; the Company ultimately retreats from the most expansive version of Wellesley’s settlement.
  • 1803–1820sThe Hyderabad Residency becomes a permanent spatial and institutional centre of indirect rule, linking Company military power to the Nizam’s court. Entry →
  • 1816–1824Palmer and Company becomes deeply entangled with Hyderabad state finance; official sanction, Metcalfe’s investigation, a large Company loan and bankruptcy expose the politics of private banking under subsidiary rule. Entry →
  • 1817–1818The Pindari campaign merges into the Third Anglo-Maratha War: Kirkee (5 November 1817), Sitabuldi at Nagpur (26–27 November), Mahidpur against Holkar (21 December), Koregaon (1 January 1818) and Ashti (20 February) break the remaining centres of armed resistance. Entry →
  • 3 June 1818Bajirao II surrenders. The Peshwaship is abolished, Satara is restored as a subordinate principality, and the Bombay government begins the administrative settlement of the conquered Deccan. Entry →
  • 1818–1831Bhil resistance and Company pacification reshape Khandesh; coercive expeditions are gradually combined with agencies, recruitment and the Bhil Corps from the mid-1820s.
  • 25 October 1819Elphinstone’s report on the conquered territories frames early Bombay policy toward village institutions, hereditary officers, revenue and law. Entry →
  • 1820s–1840sBombay’s ryotwari order is constructed experimentally: Pringle’s early measurement and assessment schemes provoke criticism and abandonment before the Wingate-Goldsmid survey settlement becomes the new model.
  • 1824The Kittur rising under Rani Chennamma contests Company control in the southern Maratha country.
  • 1826–1831Umaji Naik leads a Ramoshi insurgency around Pune; he is captured and executed in 1832.
  • 1827The Bombay Regulations commonly called the Elphinstone Code consolidate law and administration in the conquered territories. Entry →
  • 1830–1831The Nagar revolt in Mysore is followed by the assumption of direct British administration while the Wodeyar dynasty remains nominally in place. Entry →

The Settlement 1831–1875

  • 1830s–1850sColonial officials recast older watans, inams, jagirs, mirasi claims and village offices as legally classifiable rights, while local elites adapt older documentary practices to the new courts and revenue state. Entry →
  • 1835–1847The Bombay revenue survey moves from Pringle’s criticised assessments to the Goldsmid-Wingate system, based on systematic measurement, classification and long-term rates. Entry →
  • 1844–1845The Gadkari rising at Kolhapur challenges changes in military service, revenue and local authority under British intervention.
  • 1848Satara is annexed after the death of Shahaji (Appa Saheb) without a recognised natural heir; in Pune, Jotirao and Savitribai Phule open a school for girls. Entry →
  • 1852Survey disturbances in Khandesh expose conflict between ryotwari measurement, customary landholding and rural authority.
  • 1852–1863The Bombay Inam Commission investigates and adjudicates claims to alienated revenue, turning Marathi and Persian documents into a new colonial archive of legally validated property. Entry →
  • 21 May 1853Berar is assigned to British administration to secure the Hyderabad Contingent; in the same month Salar Jang I becomes diwan, beginning a long programme of administrative reform. Entry →
  • 1853–1883Salar Jang restructures Hyderabad’s districts, revenue, courts, departments and education while the state remains under British paramountcy. Entry →
  • 1853–1854Raghoji III of Nagpur dies without a natural heir; Dalhousie annexes the Bhonsle state, bringing Nagpur and much of central India under direct Company rule. Entry →
  • 1853–1863Railways reach the Deccan: the Bombay-Thane line opens in 1853, and completion of the Bhor Ghat incline in 1863 gives Bombay a direct rail link onto the plateau. Entry →
  • 1857–1858The uprising of 1857 has a fragmented Deccan geography: conspiracies and mutinies occur at Satara, Kolhapur and Nargund, and an attack on the Hyderabad Residency is repulsed, while most major Deccan rulers remain aligned with the British. Entry →
  • 1 November 1858The Crown assumes the East India Company’s governing powers; Queen Victoria’s Proclamation promises non-interference in religion and a new settlement with India’s princes. Entry →
  • 1861–1865The American Civil War drives an extraordinary cotton boom in the Bombay-Deccan economy; the boom collapses into the Bombay financial crash of 1865. Entry →
  • 1863The Summary Settlement Acts replace the Inam Commission’s case-by-case historical adjudication with a more standardised quit-rent compromise for many surviving inam claims.
  • 1867 / 1881The British government resolves in principle to restore Mysore to its dynasty in 1867; rendition to Maharaja Chamarajendra Wodeyar X follows in 1881. Entry →
  • 1873Jotirao Phule publishes Gulamgiri and founds the Satyashodhak Samaj on 24 September, creating a durable anti-caste social and political organisation in western India. Entry →
  • 1874–1875The Bombay Hereditary Offices Act comes into force in February 1875, further codifying the colonial state’s treatment of watan offices. Entry →
  • 12 May–June 1875The Deccan Riots begin at Supa and spread through parts of Poona and Ahmednagar districts. Cultivators attack moneylenders and debt records amid indebtedness, post-cotton-boom contraction, revenue pressure and unequal credit relations; the Deccan Riots Commission follows. Entry →

After the close codas and horizon

  • 1876–1878The Great Famine strikes the Deccan and much of southern India – outside the collection’s 1875 end date but an immediate sequel to the agrarian history above.
  • 1879Vasudev Balwant Phadke’s armed rising begins in the western Deccan – likewise outside the formal span.
  • 1827–1893The Deccan as the Company and Crown surveyed and mapped it: the cartographic record. Entry →
  • 1870–1900Ranade and a new generation of western Indian historians reinterpret the Maratha past; Robert Sewell’s A Forgotten Empire appears in 1900. Entry →

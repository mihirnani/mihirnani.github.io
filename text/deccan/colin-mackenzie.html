Colin Mackenzie’s collection

1799–1821 · Document · Chennai (Madras), Tamil Nadu

A survey officer and his Indian assistants gathered manuscripts, inscriptions, drawings and coins across the southern Deccan on a scale never attempted before.

Colin Mackenzie, born on the Isle of Lewis in about 1753, came to India as an engineer officer in 1783 and spent the rest of his life there. After the fall of Srirangapatna he was put in charge of the survey of Mysore, which ran from 1799 to 1810 and mapped the newly partitioned kingdom district by district. He became Surveyor-General of Madras in 1810 and the first Surveyor-General of India in 1815, and died in Calcutta on 8 May 1821.

The survey was the occasion for something larger. Mackenzie employed a staff of Indian scholars, the Telugu Brahmin brothers Kavali Venkata Boria, who died in 1803, and Venkata Lakshmaiah chief among them, together with Jain and other pandits, draughtsmen and copyists. As the surveyors moved they collected: manuscripts in Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada, Tamil, Marathi and Persian; copies of inscriptions from temple walls and copper plates; local histories and family accounts dictated by village officers; drawings of monuments, including the sculptures of Amaravati, which Mackenzie first visited in 1798 and recorded systematically between 1816 and 1820; and thousands of coins. The collection had no parallel in India. After his death the Company bought it from his widow for 100,000 rupees, and H. H. Wilson catalogued it in 1828. It is now divided between the British Library, the British Museum and the Government Museum in Chennai.

Mackenzie’s papers remain a foundation for the history of the medieval and early modern Deccan. Much of what is known about Vijayanagara’s nayakas, the poligars and the village-level working of the old revenue systems comes from the accounts his assistants took down. The collection also fixed a method. Knowledge of the conquered country, in this view, was something to be surveyed, copied and filed, like its hills and rivers.

In the story

The Mackenzie collection belongs in this story as the moment the Company began to know the Deccan’s layered past as an archive. His assistants recorded the sanads and inscriptions by which sultans, rayas, nayakas, Mughal governors and Maratha chiefs had granted and confirmed rights, and in doing so they documented the plurality of authority this collection traces. The Company used that record to adjudicate claims in the districts it now ruled. The old grants survived, but their meaning was settled by the paramount power.

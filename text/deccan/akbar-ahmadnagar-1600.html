Akbar takes Ahmadnagar

August 1600 · Event · Ahmadnagar, Maharashtra

The Mughal storming of Ahmadnagar fort, following the cession of Berar in 1596, creates the first Mughal provinces south of the Narmada.

Mughal troops under Prince Daniyal and Abdur Rahim Khan-i-Khanan stormed the fort of Ahmadnagar in August 1600, after a siege of about four months, and sent the boy sultan Bahadur Nizam Shah into captivity at Gwalior. Akbar himself had come south to Burhanpur to direct the campaign and in January 1601 took the Faruqi fortress of Asirgarh in Khandesh, which controlled the road from Malwa into the Deccan.

Akbar had first sent embassies to the four Deccan sultanates in 1591, inviting them to acknowledge his overlordship; none did. Ahmadnagar, weakest and nearest, was the obvious target. The 1596 settlement with Chand Bibi had ceded Berar, and in 1600 the Mughals added the Ahmadnagar capital and the country around it. Akbar organised the conquests as three subahs, Khandesh, Berar and Ahmadnagar, with Daniyal as viceroy, and returned north in 1601.

The conquest was incomplete. The Nizam Shahi nobility did not surrender with the fort, and within a few years Malik Ambar had installed a new sultan at Parenda and then at Junnar and was raiding the Mughal provinces from the hills. For three decades the Mughal hold on the Deccan consisted of a few fortified towns and a frontier that moved with the campaigning season. Jahangir’s generals, including his son Khurram, the future Shah Jahan, spent much of their careers there.

Even so, 1600 is the hinge of this period. Berar and Khandesh gave the empire the revenue base and the staging posts it needed, and Burhanpur and later Aurangabad became the seats from which Mughal princes governed the south. Bijapur and Golconda, which had watched Ahmadnagar fall, now had an imperial neighbour on their northern border.

In the story

Until 1600 the Deccan’s sultanates answered to no one. After it, one of the five had been partly swallowed by an empire whose scale and resources were of a different order, and the others had to calculate their own survival in relation to it. The layered sovereignty of the period that follows, Mughal provinces, tributary sultanates, Maratha deshmukhs serving several masters at once, all begins with this incomplete conquest.

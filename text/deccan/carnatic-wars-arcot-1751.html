The Carnatic wars

1746–1763 · Event · Arcot, Tamil Nadu

Three Anglo-French wars on the Coromandel coast, fought through Indian claimants, that ended with the Company as the Carnatic’s real power.

Between 1746 and 1763 the English and French East India Companies fought three wars in the Carnatic, the Mughal province south of the Nizam’s country whose nawab sat at Arcot. The first (1746–48) was an extension of the War of the Austrian Succession: Dupleix, governor of Pondicherry, took Madras in September 1746 and returned it at the peace of Aix-la-Chapelle. The second (1749–54) was fought in peacetime, as each company backed a candidate for nawab of the Carnatic and for Nizam at Hyderabad: the French Chanda Sahib and Muzaffar Jang, the English Muhammad Ali and Nasir Jang. Dupleix briefly held the upper hand, with French troops under Bussy installed at the Nizam’s court and the Northern Circars ceded to France for their pay.

The turn came at Arcot. In August 1751 Robert Clive, a twenty-five-year-old Company writer with a commission, seized the nawab’s capital with about five hundred men to draw Chanda Sahib off from Trichinopoly, and held it through a fifty-day siege. Chanda Sahib was killed in 1752, Dupleix was recalled in 1754, and the Treaty of Pondicherry recognised Muhammad Ali as nawab. The third war (1757–63) followed the Seven Years’ War: Lally besieged Madras and failed, Eyre Coote beat him at Wandiwash on 22 January 1760, and Pondicherry surrendered in January 1761. The Treaty of Paris (1763) returned the French factories but forbade them fortifications or troops.

The wars were fought in the Mughal idiom of sanads, titles and subahdars, and won by European infantry and artillery in Indian pay. They established the model of the sepoy army and of the Indian ruler who kept his throne on a European subsidy; Muhammad Ali Walajah, nawab of the Carnatic until 1795, owed his whole position to the Company and his debts to its servants. They also removed France as a competitor in the south, and left Madras free to turn its attention west, to Mysore.

In the story

The Carnatic wars are where the Company learned to use the Deccan’s layered sovereignty rather than merely survive in it. By backing claimants to Mughal offices it acquired revenue, territory and an army without claiming to rule anything. The nawab of Arcot became the first of the dependent sovereigns, and the technique perfected here was applied to the Nizam in 1798 and the Peshwa in 1802.

Mysore restored

30 June 1799 · Event · Mysore, Karnataka

Eight weeks after Tipu’s death the Company installed a five-year-old Wodeyar on a reduced Mysore throne, with Tipu’s own minister Purnaiya to run it.

On 30 June 1799 Krishnaraja Wodeyar III, born on 14 July 1794 and not yet five, was installed as raja of Mysore at the old capital of the dynasty. His family had been kept as pensioners by Haidar Ali and Tipu for nearly forty years. The Governor-General, Richard Wellesley, chose to restore them rather than annex the whole state, and partitioned what remained of Tipu’s kingdom. The Company took Kanara on the west coast, Coimbatore and Wynad in the south, and the island fortress of Srirangapatna; the Nizam received districts in the north-east around Gooty and Gurramkonda; the Peshwa was offered a share on condition he accept a subsidiary force, and declined.

The new state was bound by a subsidiary treaty signed in July 1799. Mysore paid for a Company force stationed in its territory, surrendered the conduct of its foreign relations to a Resident (the first was Barry Close), and accepted a clause allowing the Company to take over the administration if the subsidy fell into arrears or the government failed. Purnaiya, the Brahmin financier who had served Haidar and Tipu as revenue minister, was appointed dewan and regent. He governed for eleven years, keeping much of the Mysore revenue machinery intact, paying the subsidy punctually and building up a surplus, before handing power to the raja in 1810 and dying in 1812.

The settlement was Wellesley’s template. A hereditary ruler with a court and ceremony; a minister answerable in practice to the Resident; an army paid for by the state but commanded by the Company; and a reserved right of intervention. Mysore was the first state in the Deccan to be rebuilt on these terms, and the clause permitting the Company to assume the government would be used in 1831.

In the story

The restoration of 1799 shows how paramountcy was built by preserving rather than abolishing older sovereignties. The Wodeyars, a Vijayanagara-era lineage sidelined by Haidar and Tipu, were brought back as a convenient ruling house; their state kept its own revenue officers, its dewan and its ceremonial, yet its army, its borders and its foreign relations were now in the Company’s hands. The layered sovereignty of the Deccan did not vanish in 1799. It was reorganised, with one layer now plainly on top.

Tipu’s state

1784–1799 · Institution · Srirangapatna, Karnataka

The revenue regulations, state trading, new coinage, silk industry and Mauludi calendar by which Tipu tried to make Mysore a sovereign state of a new kind.

Between the Treaty of Mangalore in 1784 and his death in 1799 Tipu Sultan issued a stream of regulations that together amount to a programme of state-building. Much of what is known of them comes from his own letters and orders, edited by the Company officer William Kirkpatrick as Select Letters of Tippoo Sultan in 1811, and from the records seized in 1799, so the evidence has passed through hostile hands.

The revenue regulations, issued in the mid-1780s, set out in detail the duties of the amildar, the district officer, and aimed to collect directly from cultivators, bypass hereditary intermediaries such as the poligars, and offer remissions for bringing waste land into cultivation. A commercial department ran state trading in sandalwood, pepper, silk and other goods, with factories at Muscat and elsewhere in the Gulf. From 1786 Tipu ordered silkworms and skilled rearers from Bengal and Muscat; by 1793 there were some twenty-one breeding stations, and the Mysore silk industry dates from them. The army was reorganised into cushoons on French lines, with a rocket establishment and a navy projected but never completed.

Sovereignty was also a matter of symbols. In 1784 Tipu introduced the Mauludi era, counted from the birth of the Prophet rather than the Hijra, with a lunisolar year suited to the revenue cycle and month names of his own devising; the coinage was redesigned, struck in his own name with the new dates from 1787, and given denominations with Persian and Arabic names. The tiger became the emblem of the state, on the throne, the standards and the weapons. Brittlebank reads all this as a search for legitimacy by a ruler whose father had been a usurper and whose dynasty had no Mughal sanad.

Most of the institutions did not survive 1799. Purnaiya, who had served Tipu, carried elements of the revenue system into the restored Wodeyar state, and the silk remained; the calendar, coinage and trading factories ended with the sultan.

In the story

Tipu’s regulations are the most ambitious attempt by any Deccan ruler of the period to make a state that was sovereign in substance as well as in name: its own era, its own coin, its own trade, its own officers answerable to the centre. That ambition, more than his wars, is what made Mysore intolerable to the Company, which could accommodate nominal kings but not a rival system. The restored Mysore of 1799 kept the administrative machinery and lost the claim to sovereignty that had animated it.

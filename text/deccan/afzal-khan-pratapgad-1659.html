Pratapgad, 1659

10 November 1659 · Battle · Pratapgad, Satara district, Maharashtra

Shivaji kills the Bijapuri general Afzal Khan at a parley below Pratapgad and routs his army, ending Bijapur’s attempt to recover the Pune hills.

On 10 November 1659 Shivaji met Afzal Khan, commander of the Bijapuri army sent to suppress him, in a tent below the fort of Pratapgad in the Western Ghats. The meeting had been arranged as a parley, each man with a small escort. What happened inside is known only from partisan accounts: the Maratha chronicles say Afzal Khan embraced Shivaji and tried to stab him, and that Shivaji, wearing mail under his clothes, killed him with a concealed blade and a set of steel tiger claws. Afzal Khan’s escort was cut down, and the Maratha troops hidden in the forest fell on the Bijapuri camp in the valley and destroyed it.

Afzal Khan had marched from Bijapur in the spring with a force of perhaps ten thousand, by way of Tuljapur and Pandharpur, where he is said to have desecrated the temples to provoke Shivaji into the open. Shivaji refused battle on the plain and drew him into the hills, where cavalry was useless and the monsoon forest gave cover. The choice of ground decided the outcome before the parley began.

The consequences were immediate. Shivaji’s men captured the Bijapuri guns, horses and treasure, took Panhala on 28 November and defeated a second Bijapuri force near Kolhapur on 28 December. Bijapur, having lost its best general and an army, sent Siddi Jauhar to besiege Panhala the following year, but it never again attempted to govern the Pune hills directly, and in 1662 it made peace.

Pratapgad has been retold so many times, as treachery on one side or the other, that the sources are hard to see past. What can be said is that a sultanate that had fielded armies against the Mughals for a century lost its hold on the western Deccan in an afternoon, to a local chief whom it had not considered a sovereign at all.

In the story

The killing of Afzal Khan was the point at which a jagirdar’s rebellion became a war between states, and the first time a Deccan sultanate was beaten by a power that had grown up inside it. The collection reads the battle as a shift in what counted as a polity: after Pratapgad, Bijapur negotiated with Shivaji as an equal, and the Mughals began to treat him as a problem rather than a bandit. The tiger claws belong to legend; the change in status is documented.

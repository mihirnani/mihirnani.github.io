Albuquerque takes Goa

25 November 1510 · Event · Old Goa, Goa

Afonso de Albuquerque seized Goa from Bijapur in 1510, giving the Portuguese a permanent base and a grip on the sea-borne horse trade to the Deccan.

Afonso de Albuquerque, governor of the Portuguese Estado da Índia, first took Goa from the Bijapur sultanate on 17 February 1510, acting on the advice of the corsair Timoji, who had told him the port was poorly held. Ismail Adil Shah, newly succeeded to his father Yusuf, counter-attacked in May; the Portuguese withdrew to their ships and spent the monsoon bottled up in the Mandovi under fire. Albuquerque returned with reinforcements and retook the city on 25 November 1510, the feast of St Catherine, in a short and bloody assault. Goa would remain Portuguese until 1961.

Goa was an island port between the Konkan coast and the Western Ghats, and it had been the principal landing place for horses shipped from Hormuz and the Arabian coast, a trade on which both the sultanates and Vijayanagara depended for their cavalry. Albuquerque understood this. He ordered that all vessels carrying horses to India must unload at Goa, making the Portuguese customs house the gatekeeper of the Deccan’s most important military import, and he opened negotiations with Vijayanagara, which welcomed anyone who could deny horses to Bijapur.

The city became the seat of the viceroy, a bishopric in 1534 and an archbishopric in 1557, and the hub from which the Portuguese tried to license all shipping in the Arabian Sea through the cartaz system. It was also a place where Turkish and Persian gunners, displaced by the fighting, found employment on either side of the Krishna.

The Portuguese never became a territorial power in the Deccan, and Goa was hemmed in by Bijapur for most of the sixteenth century. But from 1510 a European state held a piece of the coast and a lever on the horse trade, and every inland ruler had to reckon with it. The Deccan had acquired a sea gate that it did not control.

In the story

Goa is the first European territorial sovereignty in the collection, and it is a small and maritime one. The Portuguese held a port, not a province; their power lay in customs, passes and horses rather than land revenue. Set beside the sultanates and Vijayanagara, Goa shows that layered sovereignty in the Deccan included a coastal layer from the start, and it foreshadows the Company, which would also begin as a presence on the shore before it became a paramount power inland.

Assaye, 1803

23 September 1803 · Battle · Assaye, Jalna district, Maharashtra

Arthur Wellesley’s small army broke the trained battalions of Scindia and Bhonsle on the Kaitna, at a cost he remembered all his life.

On 23 September 1803 Major-General Arthur Wellesley, with about 9,500 men and seventeen guns, attacked the combined armies of Daulatrao Scindia and Raghuji Bhonsle of Nagpur near the village of Assaye, between the Kaitna and Juah rivers in the northern Deccan. The Maratha force was several times larger. Its core was Scindia’s European-trained regular infantry, commanded in the field by the Hanoverian officer Anthony Pohlmann, with more than a hundred guns; the rest was irregular cavalry. Wellesley had divided his army to find the enemy, and chose to attack at once rather than wait for Stevenson’s column.

He crossed the Kaitna by an unguarded ford and struck the Maratha line in flank, forcing it to change front. The Maratha artillery was well served and the Company’s sepoy battalions and the 74th and 78th Highlanders took heavy losses before the line was carried. Company casualties were about 1,600 killed, wounded and missing, roughly a sixth of the force; Maratha losses are estimated at several thousand, and almost all their guns were taken. Wellesley called it the bloodiest action for its size that he ever saw, and late in life still ranked it above Waterloo. Wellesley beat the retreating armies again at Argaon on 29 November, and Stevenson stormed the hill fort of Gawilgarh in December.

Assaye, with Lake’s victories at Delhi and Laswari in the north, decided the second Anglo-Maratha war. Bhonsle made peace at Deogaon on 17 December 1803 and Scindia at Surji-Anjangaon on 30 December, each ceding territory and accepting a Resident. The trained infantry on which Mahadji Scindia had built his power, the Maratha answer to the Company’s sepoy army, had been tested and had lost.

In the story

Assaye tested the idea that a Maratha house could match the Company on its own terms, with drilled battalions, European officers and a train of artillery. Scindia’s army had been built for exactly this, and it fought well. Its defeat meant that the military balance on which the confederacy’s independence rested was gone. After 1803 the Maratha houses survived as subsidiary or treaty states within a Company order, and the Nizam, whose contingent marched with Wellesley, had chosen the winning side. One more layer of Deccan sovereignty had been subordinated.

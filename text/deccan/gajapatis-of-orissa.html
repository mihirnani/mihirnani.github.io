The Gajapatis of Orissa

1435–1541 · Institution · Cuttack, Odisha

The Suryavamsi kings of Orissa – Kapilendra, Purushottama, Prataparudra – pushed down the Andhra coast for eighty years and were the third power of the Deccan’s first two periods, until Krishnadevaraya drove them back to the Krishna.

Kapilendra Deva took the throne of Orissa in 1434–35 at the end of the Eastern Ganga line and gave his house the title Gajapati, lord of elephants. In the scheme the inscriptions of the age use, that was one of three sovereignties in the subcontinent, beside the Narapati, lord of men, which Vijayanagara claimed, and the Ashvapati, lord of horses, which was the sultans’ share; the title was a programme, and for a generation the Gajapatis acted on it. Kapilendra’s armies came down the coast through the Reddi and Velama country, took Rajahmundry and Kondavidu in the 1450s, intervened in the Bahmani succession, and by the 1460s were raiding far to the south. Oriya tradition and the inscriptions claim Kanchi and a tribute from the Tamil country, and Vijayanagara under Mallikarjuna could not stop them.

Purushottama (1467–97) held the Andhra conquests and lost the far south. His son Prataparudra (1497–1540) inherited a frontier on the Krishna that Vijayanagara now meant to move, and Krishnadevaraya’s eastern war of 1513–19 was the longest campaign of his reign. Udayagiri, the hill fort at the southern end of the Gajapati domain, fell in 1514 after a siege of more than a year; Kondavidu in 1515, with a Gajapati prince among the prisoners; then Kondapalli, Rajahmundry and Simhachalam, where the king had his victory recorded on the temple walls, and a march to the Gajapati capital at Cuttack. The peace of 1519 gave Vijayanagara everything south of the Krishna and gave Krishnadevaraya a Gajapati princess as a queen. The river became the frontier between the two Hindu kingdoms and, within a few years, the line along which Golconda advanced instead.

The Gajapatis did not long survive the settlement. Prataparudra’s successors were murdered in turn, a minister’s family took the throne in 1541, and the Qutb Shahs, then the Mughals, absorbed the coast the dynasty had ruled; Kondavidu and Kondapalli, which had passed from Reddi to Gajapati to Raya within a lifetime, were Golconda’s by the middle of the century.

The Gajapati century matters here because it breaks the symmetry of ‘two Deccans’. For eighty years there were three sovereignties on the plateau’s eastern edge, and the third was Hindu, claimed the same imperial titles as Vijayanagara, and fought it as readily as it fought the sultans. The coalition politics of the 1560s were rehearsed on the Krishna frontier a generation earlier, with Orissa in the third seat.

In the chronology

  • 1430s–1460sThe Gajapati rulers of Odisha expand into coastal Andhra and periodically threaten Vijayanagara and Bahmani interests, making the eastern Deccan a major three-sided frontier.

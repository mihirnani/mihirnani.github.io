Period 07 · 1831–1875

The Settlement

Paramountcy inherited five centuries of grants, offices and hereditary rights; a survey, a commission and an Act of 1874 make them legible and subordinate, and in 1875 the new order produces a crisis of its own.

Paramountcy placed one layer on top of the others; it did not remove them. The Company took over a Deccan in which thousands of people held hereditary claims to revenue, office and service – the watans of patils and kulkarnis, the inams of temples, mosques and Brahmins, the jagirs and saranjams of the Maratha military houses – some of them carrying governmental or quasi-sovereign authority and most of them confirmed by Bahmani, Adil Shahi or Peshwa sanads, and Elphinstone had deliberately left the structure standing. Between 1831 and 1875 the state worked out what to do with it, in a sequence that runs from measurement to adjudication to registration: the revenue survey put a price on every field; the Inam Commission called in every grant and asked for the paper; Satara lapsed and Berar was assigned; 1857 tested the settlement; the Crown replaced the Company and guaranteed the princes; Mysore was argued over and given back; and the Bombay Hereditary Offices Act of 1874 entered the village officers who had carried the old order in a register, fixed their shares and their turns of service, and placed them under the Collector. The colonial state did not finish the old order. It made it legible and subordinate; the abolition of the hereditary offices waited for independent India. The period, and the collection, end with the Deccan Riots of 1875, when the new combination of cash assessment, private debt and civil procedure reached the village. The first modern Indian historians of the Marathas, writing in Poona in the closing decades of the century, are the coda.

    What the Company took over with the Peshwa’s territories in 1818: watans, inams, saranjams and temple grants, left standing by Elphinstone and still unsettled in 1831.

  • 1830s–1860s Jagirs and saranjams under the Company

    The Maratha military families’ revenue assignments, and the rules by which the Company decided which would descend to heirs and which would lapse.

    Institution · East India Company · Marathas · British Crown

  • 1835–1847 The Bombay revenue survey

    Pringle’s failed survey of the Deccan, its replacement by Goldsmid and Wingate from 1835, and the Joint Report of 1847 that fixed the ryotwari settlement of Bombay.

    Institution · East India Company

  • 1848–1873 Jotirao Phule

    A Pune gardener-caste schoolmaster who opened a girls’ school in 1848, wrote Gulamgiri against Brahmin dominance, and founded the Satyashodhak Samaj in 1873 – the settlement era’s great dissenting voice.

    Person · East India Company · British Crown

  • 1848 The annexation of Satara

    The raj created for Shivaji’s descendant in 1818 ends thirty years later, when Dalhousie refuses to recognise an adopted heir and takes the state by lapse.

    Event · Marathas · East India Company

  • 1852–1863 The Inam Commission

    Act XI of 1852 set a British tribunal to test the title of every rent-free holding in the Deccan; tens of thousands of grants were examined, many reduced or resumed.

    Institution · East India Company · British Crown

  • 21 May 1853 The Berar assignment

    To clear his debts for the Contingent, the Nizam assigns Berar and other districts to the Company; Hyderabad keeps its sovereignty and loses its richest province.

    Treaty · Hyderabad · East India Company

  • 1743–1854 Nagpur, from Raghuji to the lapse

    The fourth Maratha house, which ruled Berar and the Gond country from Nagpur for a century, raided Bengal, fought the Company at Sitabaldi, and ended in December 1853 as the largest of the states taken by lapse.

    Institution · Marathas · East India Company

  • 1853–1863 The railway climbs the ghats

    India’s first passenger train ran from Bombay in 1853; ten years and a mountain later, the Bhor Ghat incline put the Deccan within a day of the port and changed what the plateau grew, owed and remembered.

    Institution · East India Company · British Crown

  • 1853–1875, ministry to 1883 Salar Jang remakes Hyderabad

    Appointed diwan at twenty-four in the year of the Berar assignment, Mir Turab Ali Khan, Salar Jang, spent thirty years building the Nizam’s state a modern administration – the Deccan’s other settlement, made from inside.

    Person · Hyderabad · British Crown · East India Company

  • 1857–1858 1857 in the Deccan

    No general rising reached the Deccan in 1857, but the year was not silent: a conspiracy at Satara, two mutinies at Kolhapur, a revolt at Nargund – and a settlement that held.

    Event · East India Company · Marathas · Hyderabad

  • 1 November 1858 The Crown takes over

    Queen Victoria’s proclamation ends Company rule, confirms every treaty with the princes and promises no further annexation; the paramount power changes its name and fixes its map.

    Document · British Crown · East India Company · Hyderabad · Mysore

  • 1861–1865 The cotton boom and bust

    The American Civil War sent the price of Deccan cotton to unheard-of heights; the crash of 1865 left debts whose collection ended in the riots of 1875.

    Event · British Crown · Hyderabad

  • 1867, rendition 1881 The Mysore question

    Whether a state taken under British administration in 1831 could be handed back: the recognition of an adopted heir in 1867 and the rendition of Mysore in 1881.

    Event · Mysore · British Crown

  • 1874, in force 5 February 1875 The Watan Act

    Bombay Act III of 1874 registers the hereditary village offices, fixes their fractional shares and brings their service under the Collector’s supervision.

    Document · British Crown

  • May–June 1875 The Deccan Riots

    Cultivators in Poona and Ahmednagar districts attack moneylenders and burn their bonds; the Commission of 1876 and the Relief Act of 1879 follow.

    Event · British Crown

    M. G. Ranade’s Rise of the Maratha Power (1900) and the Poona generation that wrote the history of what the settlement had ended, answering Grant Duff.

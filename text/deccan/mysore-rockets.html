The Mysore rocket

1780–1799 · Object · Pollilur, Tamil Nadu

Iron-cased rockets fired by Haidar and Tipu’s armies, decisive at Pollilur in 1780 and copied at Woolwich after 1799.

The rockets used by the armies of Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan were iron tubes, a little under a foot long and two or three inches across, packed with gunpowder, closed at one end and lashed to a bamboo guide-stick several feet long. Rockets were old in India and Asia; the iron casing was the Mysore refinement. Iron held higher pressure than the paper or card casings used in Europe, so the rocket flew farther, up to about a thousand yards in contemporary Company estimates, with a heavier payload. Some carried blades or small bombs. They were inaccurate singly and used in volleys, from launching frames or from camels and by hand.

At Pollilur, near Kanchipuram, on 10 September 1780, Colonel William Baillie’s column of about 3,800 men was destroyed by Haidar and Tipu’s army. Company accounts describe rockets skimming the ground into the close-packed ranks and, by several later tellings, setting off ammunition waggons. Baillie and some two hundred Europeans were taken to Srirangapatna. Tipu had the battle painted on the walls of the Daria Daulat Bagh. Rockets were used again at every engagement of 1790–92 and 1799, and Tipu’s establishment of rocket-men, the jaukan, numbered in the thousands.

After the storming of Srirangapatna in May 1799 the Company inventoried some 600 launchers, 700 serviceable rockets and 9,000 empty casings, and sent cases of them to the Royal Arsenal at Woolwich. William Congreve began work there on an improved weapon in 1804, demonstrated it in 1805, and the Congreve rocket was used at Boulogne in 1806 and Copenhagen in 1807, and later at Fort McHenry in 1814.

The rocket is the one item of Deccan technology that travelled the other way. It did not change the outcome of the Mysore wars, which were decided by sieges, supply and alliances, but it entered European armouries and the history of rocketry as a Mysore invention.

In the story

The rocket complicates the usual story of European military superiority in eighteenth-century India. Mysore’s army was not a traditional force overwhelmed by modern technique; it was a modernising state with its own ordnance, a French-drilled infantry and an artillery park the Company coveted. The Company’s eventual paramountcy rested on finance, coalitions and sea power more than on weapons, and the transfer of the rocket to Woolwich is a small but concrete sign that the flow of knowledge was not one-directional.

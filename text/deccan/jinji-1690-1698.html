The siege of Jinji

1690–1698 · Event · Jinji (Gingee), Tamil Nadu

An eight-year Mughal siege of the Maratha court in exile at Jinji, in the far south, that ended with the fort taken and the king gone.

After Sambhaji was captured and executed in 1689, his younger brother Rajaram escaped from Raigad and made his way, disguised and by stages, to the hill fort of Jinji (Gingee) in the Tamil country, a Maratha possession since Shivaji’s southern campaign of 1677. From there he claimed the Maratha kingship. Aurangzeb sent Zulfiqar Khan to reduce the place, and the siege opened in September 1690. It lasted, with long intermissions, until 8 January 1698, when the fort fell and was renamed Nusratgarh.

Jinji is three rocky hills joined by walls, and it was well provisioned. Zulfiqar Khan’s army was large but its supply lines ran the length of the peninsula, and the Maratha commanders Santaji Ghorpade and Dhanaji Jadhav, operating in the open country of the Deccan and the Carnatic, harried its convoys and defeated detachments sent against them. Zulfiqar Khan was in any case in no hurry; contemporaries and later historians suspected that he preferred a long siege to a decisive one, since a Maratha king in being kept him in command of a great army far from the emperor. Rajaram slipped out of the fort before the final assault, late in 1697, reached Vellore and then Vishalgad, and resumed the war from the western hills. Santaji was murdered in 1697 in a factional quarrel; Rajaram died at Sinhagad in March 1700.

The siege mattered more for what it failed to do than for its result. Aurangzeb had, by 1689, removed both remaining sultanates and the Maratha king, and might reasonably have expected the Deccan war to end. Instead a court in exile eight hundred kilometres from Raigad kept a Maratha sovereignty alive, and while the emperor’s best general sat before Jinji the Maratha cavalry learned to live off Mughal territory. The pattern of the next decade – a Mughal army that could take any fort and hold no countryside – was set here.

In the story

Jinji shows a kingdom as something portable. The Maratha kingdom of the 1690s had no fixed capital, a king in a borrowed fort at the far end of the peninsula, and commanders who raised their own revenue in the field. That the state survived in this form, and that Mughal generals found it convenient not to end it, says a good deal about how thin imperial control of the Deccan remained even at the moment of Aurangzeb’s greatest territorial reach.

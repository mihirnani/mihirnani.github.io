Readings

The standard scholarship

An annotated bibliography, general first and then by period.

General

  • C. A. Bayly, Indian Society and the Making of the British Empire (The New Cambridge History of India, II.1) (Cambridge University Press, 1988)

    A compact survey of how the Company state grew out of the eighteenth-century successor polities and the Indian commercial and military world around them. The best short framing for periods 3 to 5.

  • Lauren Benton, Law and Colonial Cultures: Legal Regimes in World History, 1400–1900 (Cambridge University Press, 2002)

    Comparative background for jurisdictional pluralism and the movement from overlapping legal orders toward more hierarchical colonial regimes.

  • Lauren Benton, A Search for Sovereignty: Law and Geography in European Empires, 1400–1900 (Cambridge University Press, 2009)

    Provides a comparative vocabulary for corridors, enclaves, irregular jurisdiction and differentiated imperial space. Use comparatively, not as an interpretive template into which Deccan evidence must fit.

  • Lauren Benton and Richard J. Ross (eds.), Legal Pluralism and Empires, 1500–1850 (New York University Press, 2013)

    Useful comparative essays on jurisdiction, composite polities and the political imagination of plural law.

  • Richard M. Eaton, A Social History of the Deccan, 1300–1761: Eight Indian Lives (The New Cambridge History of India, I.8) (Cambridge University Press, 2005)

    Eight biographies – among them Rama Raya, Malik Ambar, Tukaram, Papadu and Tarabai – used to carry a social history of the plateau from the Tughluq conquest to Panipat. The single best introduction to the whole span of this site.

  • Roy S. Fischel, Local States in an Imperial World: Identity, Society and Politics in the Early Modern Deccan (Edinburgh University Press, 2020)

    A study of Bijapur and Golconda as states built on competing immigrant and local elites rather than as pale copies of Mughal or Persian models. The most recent monograph on the sultanates as polities.

  • Stewart Gordon, The Marathas 1600–1818 (The New Cambridge History of India, II.4) (Cambridge University Press, 1993)

    The standard one-volume English history of the Maratha polity from the Deccan sultanate service families to the fall of the Peshwa. Strong on revenue and military organisation; the obvious spine for periods 2 to 5.

  • Dirk H. A. Kolff, Naukar, Rajput, and Sepoy: The Ethnohistory of the Military Labour Market in Hindustan, 1450–1850 (Cambridge University Press, 1990)

    A northern-Indian study, but a valuable comparator for military service, mobile warrior labour and the limits of assuming fixed ethnic-state identities.

  • John F. Richards, The Mughal Empire (The New Cambridge History of India, I.5) (Cambridge University Press, 1993)

    The standard survey of the empire from Babur to the 1720s. The chapters on Aurangzeb's Deccan wars and the collapse after 1707 are the necessary imperial context for periods 2 and 3.

  • Haroon Khan Sherwani, The Bahmanis of the Deccan: An Objective Study (Saood Manzil, Hyderabad, 1953)

    The classic political narrative of the Bahmani sultanate to its break-up, drawn from the Persian chronicles. Dated in approach but still the fullest account of the dynasty from which the five successor states emerged.

  • Haroon Khan Sherwani, History of the Qutb Shahi Dynasty (Munshiram Manoharlal, 1974)

    A large political history of Golconda from Sultan Quli to the Mughal conquest of 1687. Remains the only full-length English treatment of the dynasty.

  • Burton Stein, Vijayanagara (The New Cambridge History of India, I.2) (Cambridge University Press, 1989)

    A short interpretative history of the empire, arguing for a segmentary state with a strong military core. Brief, and contested in its model, but the usual starting point.

  • Philip J. Stern, The Company-State: Corporate Sovereignty and the Early Modern Foundations of the British Empire in India (Oxford University Press, 2011)

    Essential for the Company's status as a political and sovereign body before territorial conquest, deriving authority from overlapping European and Asian grants, jurisdictions and practices.

  • Sanjay Subrahmanyam, Penumbral Visions: Making Polities in Early Modern South India (University of Michigan Press, 2001)

    Linked essays on the making and unmaking of South Indian states from the sixteenth century to the Carnatic Nawabs and Mysore, read against Portuguese, Dutch and Persian sources. Good on the coastal and Tamil sides of the story.

  • Richard M. Eaton and Phillip B. Wagoner, Power, Memory, Architecture: Contested Sites on India's Deccan Plateau, 1300–1600 (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 2014)

    Reads the frontier towns of Kalyana, Raichur and Warangal through their buildings and inscriptions to show how Vijayanagara and the sultanates shared a single political culture. Essential for period 1 and for the idea of a Deccan rather than two Deccans.

  • André Wink, Land and Sovereignty in India: Agrarian Society and Politics under the Eighteenth-Century Maratha Svarājya (Cambridge University Press, 1986)

    A structural account of Maratha sovereignty as a system of shared rights, co-sharing (vatan) and managed sedition (fitna). Difficult, but the most influential modern analysis of how the Maratha state actually worked.

  • H. K. Sherwani and P. M. Joshi (eds), History of Medieval Deccan (1295–1724), 2 vols (Government of Andhra Pradesh, Hyderabad, 1973)

    A two-volume collaborative history, the first volume political and the second on administration, society, religion, literature and the arts. The standard reference compilation on the sultanate Deccan; the second volume appeared in 1974.

Two Foundations, c. 1330–1500

  • Isami, trans. Agha Mahdi Husain, Futuhu's Salatin, or Shah Namah-i Hind of Isami: Translation and Commentary, 3 vols (Asia Publishing House, Bombay, 1967–77)

    English translation with commentary of the Persian verse chronicle completed at Daulatabad in 1350 for the first Bahmani sultan, Ala al-Din Hasan Bahman Shah. The foundational insider narrative for the Tughluq occupation of the Deccan and the rebellion that produced the Bahmani state, and openly hostile to Muhammad bin Tughluq.

  • Ibn Battuta, trans. H. A. R. Gibb and C. F. Beckingham, The Travels of Ibn Battuta, A.D. 1325–1354, vol. IV (Hakluyt Society, Second Series, no. 178) (Hakluyt Society, London, 1994)

    The Indian volume of the standard annotated English translation, covering the traveller's years in Tughluq service and his journey south through Daulatabad to the Konkan and Honavar. The only contemporary eyewitness account of the Deccan in the 1340s, on the eve of the Bahmani revolt.

  • R. H. Major (ed.), India in the Fifteenth Century: Being a Collection of Narratives of Voyages to India in the Century Preceding the Portuguese Discovery of the Cape of Good Hope (Hakluyt Society, First Series, no. 22) (Hakluyt Society, London, 1857)

    Gathers the fifteenth-century travellers in English translation – Abdur Razzaq's Persian embassy to Vijayanagara in 1442–43, Nicolo Conti, Afanasy Nikitin's Russian account of Bidar and the Bahmani court in the 1470s, and Santo Stefano. Old and unevenly translated but still the most convenient single volume, and freely available online.

  • H. K. Sherwani, Mahmud Gawan, the Great Bahmani Wazir (Kitabistan, Allahabad, 1942)

    The only full-length biography of the Persian merchant-statesman who dominated the Bidar sultanate from the 1450s until his execution in 1481, drawing on his own letter collection, the Riyaz al-Insha. Dated in method but indispensable for the administrative reforms and the Afaqi–Dakhni factional struggle that destroyed him.

  • Ghulam Yazdani, Bidar: Its History and Monuments (Oxford University Press, London (for the Nizam's Government, Hyderabad), 1947)

    The Hyderabad Archaeological Department's definitive survey of the second Bahmani capital – fort, palaces, Mahmud Gawan's madrasa, the royal tombs at Ashtur – with plans, photographs and the inscriptions. Still the primary reference for the buildings; later reprinted by Motilal Banarsidass.

  • Richard M. Eaton, A Social History of the Deccan, 1300–1761: Eight Indian Lives (The New Cambridge History of India I.8) (Cambridge University Press, 2005)

    A history of the plateau told through eight biographies; the first three – Pratapa Rudra of Warangal, the Sufi Muhammad Gisu Daraz at Gulbarga, and Mahmud Gawan – cover the Tughluq conquest, the Bahmani court and the Deccani–Westerner conflict. The best single starting point for the period.

  • Peter Jackson, The Delhi Sultanate: A Political and Military History (Cambridge Studies in Islamic Civilization) (Cambridge University Press, 1999)

    The standard modern political history of the sultanate to 1400, with a critical reading of the Persian chronicles. Essential for Muhammad bin Tughluq's Deccan policy, the transfer of the capital to Daulatabad and the loss of the south in the 1340s.

  • Noboru Karashima, Towards a New Formation: South Indian Society under Vijayanagar Rule (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 1992)

    Inscription-based study of the nayaka system, temple land and rural society in the Tamil country under Vijayanagara, arguing for a new, more feudal social formation. The principal counter-position to Stein's segmentary state.

  • Hermann Kulke, 'Maharajas, Mahants and Historians: Reflections on the Historiography of Early Vijayanagara and Sringeri', in A. L. Dallapiccola and S. Zingel-Avé Lallemant (eds), Vijayanagara – City and Empire: New Currents of Research, vol. 1 (Franz Steiner, Stuttgart, 1985)

    Dismantles the Vidyaranya foundation legend by showing that the Sringeri matha's claim to have inspired the city rests on later copper plates and sixteenth-century tradition. The necessary corrective before reading any account of the Sangama brothers and the founding of 1336.

  • Elizabeth Schotten Merklinger, Indian Islamic Architecture: The Deccan 1347–1686 (Aris & Phillips, Warminster, 1981)

    Concise architectural history with a chronological catalogue of monuments and inscriptions, strongest on the Bahmani mosques and tombs of Gulbarga and Bidar. Useful as a dated checklist rather than an interpretative study.

  • Burton Stein, Peasant State and Society in Medieval South India (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 1980)

    The segmentary-state model of South Indian kingship, carried from the Cholas into the Vijayanagara period, with a long chapter on the fourteenth- and fifteenth-century transition. Much contested but still the framework every later study of Vijayanagara argues with.

  • Cynthia Talbot, Precolonial India in Practice: Society, Region, and Identity in Medieval Andhra (Oxford University Press, New York, 2001)

    Epigraphic study of Kakatiya Andhra and its aftermath, the best account of the warrior lineages – Reddis, Velamas and their rivals – who filled the space between Warangal's fall in 1323 and the consolidation of Vijayanagara and the Bahmanis. Also contains her influential reading of the 'Sultan among Hindu kings' inscriptions.

  • Richard M. Eaton and Phillip B. Wagoner, Power, Memory, Architecture: Contested Sites on India's Deccan Plateau, 1300–1600 (Oxford University Press, New Delhi, 2014)

    Reads the frontier between Vijayanagara and the sultanates through the fortified towns that changed hands along it – Kalyana, Raichur, Warangal – and shows how both sides drew on a shared courtly and architectural vocabulary. Good for the Raichur doab and for dismantling the Hindu–Muslim frontier model.

  • Phillip B. Wagoner, Tidings of the King: A Translation and Ethnohistorical Analysis of the Rayavacakamu (University of Hawaii Press, Honolulu, 1993)

    Translation of a Telugu court text on Krishnadevaraya with an analysis of how Vijayanagara remembered its own origins and its relationship with the 'Turuka' sultanates. Useful for the ideology of the founding and for reading later Telugu memory back against the fifteenth century.

Two Deccans, 1500–1565

  • Muhammad Qasim Firishta, translated by John Briggs, History of the Rise of the Mahomedan Power in India, till the Year A.D. 1612, 4 vols (Longman, Rees, Orme, Brown, and Green, London, 1829)

    Briggs's translation of the Tarikh-i Firishta, written at Bijapur around 1610 and still the main narrative source for the Bahmanis and the successor sultanates. The translation is loose and abridged but has been the working text in English for two centuries.

  • Emma J. Flatt, The Courts of the Deccan Sultanates: Living Well in the Persian Cosmopolis (Cambridge University Press, 2019)

    Essential for understanding Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda as political, intellectual and ethical worlds connected to the Persian cosmopolis rather than merely as dynastic precursors to later states.

  • Phillip B. Wagoner, Tidings of the King: A Translation and Ethnohistorical Analysis of the Rāyavācakamu (University of Hawaii Press, 1993)

    A translation of a Telugu court text that presents Krishnadevaraya's reign as it was remembered at the Madurai Nayaka court around 1600, with a long analysis of its political ideas. The best way into the indigenous view of Vijayanagara kingship.

  • Robert Sewell, A Forgotten Empire (Vijayanagar): A Contribution to the History of India (Swan Sonnenschein, London, 1900)

    A narrative history of Vijayanagara with full translations of the Portuguese chronicles of Domingos Paes and Fernão Nunes, who saw the city in the 1520s and 1530s. Read it for the eyewitness accounts rather than the narrative.

  • Henry Cousens, Bijapur and its Architectural Remains, with an Historical Outline of the Adil Shahi Dynasty (Archaeological Survey of India, Imperial Series XXXVII) (Government Central Press, Bombay, 1916)

    The Survey's monograph on Bijapur: a building-by-building record of the city with an outline of the dynasty drawn from Firishta and the inscriptions. Dated in its judgements but still the fullest architectural inventory, and the source of most later plans and measurements.

  • Richard M. Eaton, Sufis of Bijapur 1300–1700: Social Roles of Sufis in Medieval India (Princeton University Press, 1978)

    A study of the Sufi lineages of Bijapur and their shifting relations with the Adil Shahi court, the countryside and Dakhni vernacular culture. Still the standard work on religion in a sultanate capital.

  • John M. Fritz and George Michell, with photographs by John Gollings, City of Victory: Vijayanagara, the Medieval Hindu Capital of Southern India (Aperture, New York, 1991)

    An illustrated account of the capital by the leaders of the Vijayanagara Research Project, organised around the royal and sacred centres. The most accessible summary of the archaeological survey of Hampi.

  • George Michell, Architecture and Art of Southern India: Vijayanagara and the Successor States 1350–1750 (The New Cambridge History of India, I.6) (Cambridge University Press, 1995)

    A survey of temple, palace and fort building under Vijayanagara and the Nayaka courts, with a shorter treatment of sculpture and painting. The companion to Stein's political volume.

  • Velcheru Narayana Rao, David Shulman and Sanjay Subrahmanyam, Symbols of Substance: Court and State in Nāyaka Period Tamilnadu (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 1992)

    An account of the Nayaka kingdoms of Madurai, Tanjavur and Senji after 1565, read through Telugu and Tamil court literature. Argues for a distinctive, commercial and theatrical style of kingship in the post-Vijayanagara south.

  • Srinivas Reddy, Raya: Krishnadevaraya of Vijayanagara (Juggernaut Books, New Delhi, 2020)

    A short life of Krishnadevaraya (r. 1509–1529) for general readers by a translator of his Telugu poem the Amuktamalyada, drawing on the inscriptions, the Portuguese visitors and the king's own verse. The only recent biography in English, and a readable way into the reign before turning to Sewell and Wagoner.

  • Sanjay Subrahmanyam, The Political Economy of Commerce: Southern India 1500–1650 (Cambridge University Press, 1990)

    A study of trade, ports and state finance on the Coromandel and western coasts in the Vijayanagara and Nayaka period, drawing on Portuguese and Dutch records. The standard economic history for period 2.

  • Cynthia Talbot, Precolonial India in Practice: Society, Region, and Identity in Medieval Andhra (Oxford University Press, New York, 2001)

    A social history of Kakatiya and post-Kakatiya Andhra built from thousands of temple inscriptions. Strictly earlier than 1500, but the best account of the Telugu warrior society that Vijayanagara and Golconda inherited.

  • Phillip B. Wagoner, 'Sultan among Hindu Kings': Dress, Titles, and the Islamicization of Hindu Culture at Vijayanagara, Journal of Asian Studies 55 (4), pp. 851–880 (Association for Asian Studies, 1996)

    The article that reframed Vijayanagara as a participant in Islamicate court culture, through the kings' title 'sultan among Hindu kings' and the adoption of Persianate dress. Short and much cited.

  • George Michell and Mark Zebrowski, Architecture and Art of the Deccan Sultanates (The New Cambridge History of India, I.7) (Cambridge University Press, 1999)

    The standard survey of sultanate building at Gulbarga, Bidar, Bijapur, Golconda and Ahmadnagar, with Zebrowski's chapters on Deccani painting and decorative arts.

  • Navina Najat Haidar and Marika Sardar (eds), Sultans of Deccan India, 1500–1700: Opulence and Fantasy (The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, 2015)

    The catalogue of the Metropolitan Museum exhibition, with essays on each sultanate court and full entries on painting, textiles, metalwork and architecture. The standard visual reference for the sultanates.

The Mughal South, 1565–1687

  • Jos J. L. Gommans, Mughal Warfare: Indian Frontiers and High Roads to Empire, 1500–1700 (Routledge, 2002)

    Particularly useful for cavalry, military labour, communications, frontiers and the physical mechanics of projecting power.

  • Irfan Habib, The Agrarian System of Mughal India, 1556–1707 (Oxford University Press, 2nd revised edition, 1999)

    Fundamental for land revenue, zamindars, jagirdars, agrarian production and the fiscal foundations of Mughal power. The second edition (1999) remains widely cited; use one edition consistently.

  • Farhat Hasan, State and Locality in Mughal India: Power Relations in Western India, c.1572–1730 (Cambridge University Press, 2004)

    Crucial for seeing imperial sovereignty as a process negotiated through locality, officials, merchants and local power-holders rather than merely as a structure emanating from court.

  • Om Prakash, European Commercial Enterprise in Pre-Colonial India (The New Cambridge History of India, II.5) (Cambridge University Press, 1998)

    The best broad synthesis for placing European companies inside rather than outside Indian commercial and political systems.

  • Krishnaji Anant Sabhasad, translated by Surendra Nath Sen, Siva Chhatrapati: Being a Translation of Sabhasad Bakhar with Extracts from Chitnis and Sivadigvijaya, with Notes (University of Calcutta, 1920)

    The earliest Marathi chronicle of Shivaji, written at Rajaram's court in 1697, in the standard English translation. The nearest thing to a contemporary Maratha narrative of the reign.

  • Bhimsen Saxena, translated by Jadunath Sarkar, edited by V. G. Khobrekar, Tarikh-i-Dilkasha (Memoirs of Bhimsen relating to Aurangzib's Deccan Campaigns) (Department of Archives, Maharashtra, Bombay, 1972)

    The memoir of a Hindu officer who served in the Mughal Deccan from the 1670s to 1707, translated by Sarkar and published for his centenary. A rare eyewitness view of the campaigns against Bijapur, Golconda and the Marathas from inside the imperial army.

  • Sumit Guha, History and Collective Memory in South Asia, 1200–2000 (University of Washington Press, 2019)

    A long view of how South Asians kept and argued over the past, with substantial chapters on Maratha chronicles and the memory of Shivaji. Useful for thinking about the bakhars and their later uses.

  • Gajanan Bhaskar Mehendale, Shivaji: His Life and Times (Param Mitra Publications, Thane, 2011)

    A large, closely documented biography by a Marathi scholar who works directly from the Persian, Marathi, Portuguese and English records, with detailed discussion of the sources for every episode. The most thorough modern life of Shivaji in English.

  • John F. Richards, Mughal Administration in Golconda (Clarendon Press, Oxford, 1975)

    A study of how the Mughals absorbed Golconda after 1687 and of the Telugu zamindars, diamond revenues and frontier officers they had to manage. The key work on what conquest actually meant on the ground.

  • Jadunath Sarkar, House of Shivaji: Studies and Documents on Maratha History, Royal Period (S. N. Sarkar, Calcutta, 1940)

    Sarkar's collected essays and translated documents on Shivaji, Sambhaji, Rajaram and Shahu, including Persian and Portuguese material not used in the biography. A useful companion to Shivaji and His Times.

  • Jadunath Sarkar, Shivaji and His Times (M. C. Sarkar and Sons, Calcutta, 1919)

    The first critical biography, built on Persian, Marathi and European sources, and revised by Sarkar through several editions to 1952. Superseded on many points by Mehendale but still a readable and influential narrative.

  • Sanjay Subrahmanyam, Explorations in Connected History: From the Tagus to the Ganges (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 2005)

    Essays on Portuguese, Mughal and Deccan encounters in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, including the sultanates' dealings with Goa and the Persian world. A good corrective to purely inland accounts of the period.

The Maratha Century, 1687–1761

  • Muzaffar Alam, The Crisis of Empire in Mughal North India: Awadh and Punjab, 1707–48 (Oxford University Press, 2nd edition, 2013)

    Geographically outside the Deccan but historiographically indispensable for understanding regionalisation without equating it with instantaneous imperial disappearance.

  • Satish Chandra, Parties and Politics at the Mughal Court, 1707–1740 (Oxford University Press, 4th edition, 2002)

    First published 1959. Useful background to post-Aurangzeb court politics and the imperial context in which the Nizam and Maratha claims developed.

  • James Grant Duff, A History of the Mahrattas, 3 vols (Longman, Rees, Orme, Brown, and Green, London, 1826)

    Written by the first British Resident at Satara from Maratha family papers and oral testimony that have since partly vanished. Still the foundation of the English narrative of the Maratha century, and a primary source in its own right for how the Company understood the power it had just defeated.

  • P. J. Marshall (ed.), The Eighteenth Century in Indian History: Evolution or Revolution? (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 2003)

    A reader of the key articles in the debate over whether the eighteenth century was a period of decline or of regional growth, with a long editorial introduction. The quickest way into the historiography of the post-Mughal successor states.

  • V. G. Dighe, Peshwa Bajirao I and Maratha Expansion (Karnatak Publishing House, Bombay, 1944)

    A political and military study of Bajirao's campaigns into Malwa, Gujarat and the Nizam's dominions from the Peshwa daftar. The older standard account, now read alongside Kulkarni.

  • Munis D. Faruqui, At Empire's End: The Nizam, Hyderabad and Eighteenth-Century India, Modern Asian Studies 43 (1), pp. 5–43 (Cambridge University Press, 2009)

    An article on Nizam-ul-Mulk's withdrawal to the Deccan and the strategies by which he built a successor state while keeping Mughal forms. The best short account of the foundation of Hyderabad.

  • Hiroshi Fukazawa, The Medieval Deccan: Peasants, Social Systems and States, Sixteenth to Eighteenth Centuries (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 1991)

    Collected essays on village society, rural servants, land rights and the Maratha revenue system, drawn from Marathi documents. The standard agrarian history of the Maratha country.

  • Stewart Gordon, Marathas, Marauders, and State Formation in Eighteenth-Century India (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 1994)

    Essays on Maratha expansion into Malwa and the north, on forts, tribute and the meaning of 'plunder'. Complements the Cambridge volume with detailed case studies.

  • Sumit Guha, Environment and Ethnicity in India, 1200–1991 (Cambridge University Press, 1999)

    A study of forest and hill peoples of the western Deccan and their relations with the Maratha and later states, including the Bhils and Kolis. Useful for the margins of the Maratha polity that political histories leave out.

  • Uday S. Kulkarni, The Era of Bajirao: An Account of the Empire of the Deccan (Mula Mutha Publishers, Pune, 2016)

    A narrative of Bajirao I's career from 1720 to 1740, based on the Marathi and Persian correspondence, with maps of each campaign. Detailed and sympathetic; the fullest recent account in English.

  • Uday S. Kulkarni, The Extraordinary Epoch of Nanasaheb Peshwa (Mula Mutha Publishers, Pune, 2020)

    A long chronicle of the reign of Balaji Bajirao (1740–1761), covering the Carnatic and Bengal campaigns, the conflict with the Nizam and the building of Pune. Useful for its extensive quotation from the Peshwa daftar letters.

  • Uday S. Kulkarni, Solstice at Panipat, 14 January 1761: An Authentic Account of the Campaign of Panipat (Mula Mutha Publishers, Pune, 2011)

    A campaign history of the third battle of Panipat, reconstructed from the Marathi letters and the Persian accounts. The most detailed modern narrative of the battle and its aftermath.

  • Rosalind O'Hanlon, Letters Home: Banaras Pandits and the Maratha Regions in Early Modern India, Modern Asian Studies 44 (2), pp. 201–240 (Cambridge University Press, 2010)

    An article on the Brahman scholars who moved between the Deccan and Banaras in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and on what their letters show of Maratha patronage. A good entry to O'Hanlon's work on the Brahman ecumene and the Peshwa state.

  • G. S. Sardesai, New History of the Marathas, 3 vols (Phoenix Publications, Bombay, 1946)

    The fullest narrative history of the Marathas in English, by the editor of the Peshwa daftar selections; volume 2 (1707–1772) and volume 3 (1772–1848) appeared in 1948. Dated in tone but indispensable for detail.

  • Jadunath Sarkar, Fall of the Mughal Empire, 4 vols (M. C. Sarkar and Sons, Calcutta, 1932)

    Sarkar's narrative of the empire from Nadir Shah to 1803, published between 1932 and 1950; the first two volumes cover the Maratha wars in the north, Panipat and the Nizam. Old-fashioned but still the most detailed political narrative of the mid-eighteenth century.

  • Jadunath Sarkar, History of Aurangzib, Mainly Based on Persian Sources, 5 vols (M. C. Sarkar and Sons, Calcutta, 1912)

    The five volumes appeared between 1912 and 1924; volumes 4 and 5 narrate the conquest of Bijapur and Golconda and the long war against the Marathas to 1707. Still the standard account of Aurangzeb's Deccan.

  • Surendra Nath Sen, The Military System of the Marathas (The Book Company, Calcutta, 1928)

    A study of Maratha armies, forts, navy and pay from Shivaji to the third Anglo-Maratha war. Superseded in parts but still the standard reference on the subject.

  • C. U. Wills, British Relations with the Nagpur State in the 18th Century: An Account, Mainly Based on Contemporary English Records (Government Press, Nagpur, 1926)

    The only sustained study of the Nagpur Bhonsles before 1803, from the Company's side of the correspondence; dated in outlook, indispensable for the facts of Raghuji's house.

Mysore and the Company Wars, 1761–1799

  • C. A. Bayly, Rulers, Townsmen and Bazaars: North Indian Society in the Age of British Expansion, 1770–1870 (Cambridge University Press, 1983)

    Northern Indian in emphasis but conceptually invaluable on merchants, information, credit and the political economy of state formation.

  • Tipu Sultan, translated by William Kirkpatrick, Select Letters of Tippoo Sultan to Various Public Functionaries (Black, Parry, and Kingsbury, London, 1811)

    Translations of some four hundred of Tipu's official letters of 1785–1786, taken from Seringapatam in 1799 and published with hostile commentary by the Company's Persian translator. The main body of Tipu's own words in English, to be read with the editor's bias in mind.

  • Pamela Nightingale, Trade and Empire in Western India, 1784–1806 (Cambridge University Press, 1970)

    Important for the Bombay Presidency, commercial interests and imperial expansion before and during the Second Anglo-Maratha War.

  • Sarojini Regani, Nizam–British Relations, 1724–1857 (Concept Publishing, New Delhi, 1988)

    A narrative of the Nizam's dealings with the Company from Nizam-ul-Mulk to 1857, written from the Hyderabad and Residency records: the Circars, the subsidiary treaties of 1798 and 1800, the Contingent and the Berar assignment. The standard reference for the treaty sequence the entries on Hyderabad follow.

  • Lakshmi Subramanian, Indigenous Capital and Imperial Expansion: Bombay, Surat and the West Coast (Oxford University Press, 1996)

    Essential for merchants, credit, Bombay and the Indian commercial networks through which Company power expanded.

  • Robert Travers, Ideology and Empire in Eighteenth-Century India: The British in Bengal (Cambridge University Press, 2007)

    Bengal is the case study, but the conceptual problem is directly relevant: how Company rulers used and reconstructed Mughal forms to legitimate new authority.

  • Mark Wilks, Historical Sketches of the South of India, in an Attempt to Trace the History of Mysoor, 3 vols (Longman, Hurst, Rees, and Orme, London, 1810)

    A history of Mysore by the Company's Resident, published in three volumes between 1810 and 1817, from Kannada and Persian records and from interviews with survivors of Haidar's and Tipu's administrations. The foundation of all later English accounts of the Mysore state.

  • Irfan Habib (ed.), Confronting Colonialism: Resistance and Modernization under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan (Anthem Press, London, 2002)

    A collection of papers from the Indian History Congress on the Mysore state's economy, army, diplomacy and technology, with an introduction by Habib. The standard statement of the 'modernising' reading of Tipu; the Anthem edition follows an Indian printing of 1999.

  • Kate Brittlebank, Tipu Sultan's Search for Legitimacy: Islam and Kingship in a Hindu Domain (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 1997)

    A study of Tipu's coins, titles, tiger emblems, temple grants and dealings with Sufis as instruments of kingship. The book that moved the argument away from the question of whether Tipu was a bigot.

  • Kate Brittlebank, Tiger: The Life of Tipu Sultan (Juggernaut, New Delhi, 2016)

    A short, measured biography for general readers by the author of the scholarly study above. The best brief introduction to Tipu.

  • Mohibbul Hasan, History of Tipu Sultan (Bibliophile, Calcutta, 1951)

    The first full scholarly biography, drawing on Persian sources and Company records, and sympathetic to its subject. Still the standard narrative life.

  • Uday S. Kulkarni, The Mastery of Hindustan: Triumphs and Travails of Madhavrao Peshwa (Mula Mutha Publishers, Pune, 2022)

    A narrative of the Maratha recovery after Panipat under Madhavrao I (1761–1772), including the wars with Haidar Ali and the Nizam and the return to Delhi. Continues the author's series from the Peshwa daftar.

  • Uday S. Kulkarni, Raghoba: The Assassination of Narayan Rao Peshwa (Mula Mutha Publishers, Pune, 2024)

    A short study of the murder of Narayanrao in 1773, the trial evidence against Raghunathrao and the Barabhai regency that followed, which led into the first Anglo-Maratha war. Useful for the politics of the Pune court in the 1770s.

  • Kate Teltscher, India Inscribed: European and British Writing on India 1600–1800 (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 1995)

    A literary study of European accounts of India, with a final chapter on the captivity narratives and propaganda that made Tipu a British villain. Good on how the Mysore wars were written up for readers at home.

  • Kaveh Yazdani, India, Modernity and the Great Divergence: Mysore and Gujarat (17th to 19th C.) (Brill, Leiden, 2017)

    A long comparative study of the economy, technology and institutions of Mysore under Haidar and Tipu set against Gujarat and against Europe. Heavy going but the most thorough recent treatment of the Mysore state's economic base.

Paramountcy, 1799–1831

  • Lauren Benton and Lisa Ford, Rage for Order: The British Empire and the Origins of International Law, 1800–1850 (Harvard University Press, 2016)

    Best used for the later transition: how officials increasingly tried to order plural jurisdictions and peripheral violence within a more coherent imperial hierarchy.

  • Francis Buchanan, A Journey from Madras through the Countries of Mysore, Canara, and Malabar, 3 vols (T. Cadell and W. Davies, London, 1807)

    The report of the survey Wellesley commissioned in 1800 of the territories taken from Tipu, recording agriculture, crafts, castes and local history district by district. The first of the great Company surveys and a primary source for the southern Deccan at the moment of conquest.

  • Mountstuart Elphinstone, Report on the Territories Conquered from the Paishwa (Government Gazette Press, Calcutta, 1821)

    Elphinstone's account, written in 1819, of the Peshwa's former dominions and of how he proposed to govern them with minimal change. The founding document of the Bombay Deccan settlement and a description of the late Maratha state by a close observer.

  • Kenneth Ballhatchet, Social Policy and Social Change in Western India, 1817–1830 (Oxford University Press, London, 1957)

    A study of Elphinstone's and Malcolm's Bombay administration, its treatment of Brahmans, inam holders, law and education in the former Peshwa's territories. The standard account of the first years of British rule in the Maratha Deccan.

  • C. A. Bayly, Empire and Information: Intelligence Gathering and Social Communication in India, 1780–1870 (Cambridge University Press, 1996)

    On the Indian information order the Company inherited and the surveyors, spies and newswriters it depended on. Relevant here for the Mysore and Maratha wars and for the surveys that followed them.

  • T. H. Beaglehole, Thomas Munro and the Development of Administrative Policy in Madras, 1792–1818 (Cambridge University Press, 1966)

    The administrative history of ryotwari from the Baramahal and the Ceded Districts to its adoption by Madras; narrower and more exact than Stein's biography on how the settlements were actually made.

  • Bernard S. Cohn, Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge: The British in India (Princeton University Press, 1996)

    Essays on the Company's investigative modes – language, law, survey, census, collecting – as instruments of rule. The theoretical frame behind much of the work on surveys and settlements in this period.

  • Randolf G. S. Cooper, The Anglo-Maratha Campaigns and the Contest for India: The Struggle for Control of the South Asian Military Economy (Cambridge University Press, 2003)

    A military history of the 1803 campaigns, including Assaye and Laswari, arguing that the Maratha armies were modern and well led and that the Company won by buying their officers and suppliers. The standard modern study of the second Anglo-Maratha war.

  • Prachi Deshpande, Creative Pasts: Historical Memory and Identity in Western India, 1700–1960 (Columbia University Press, 2007)

    A history of Marathi historical writing from the bakhars through colonial scholarship to modern regional identity. The best account of what happened to the Maratha past after 1818.

  • Nicholas B. Dirks, Castes of Mind: Colonialism and the Making of Modern India (Princeton University Press, 2001)

    Argues that caste as a single all-India system was a colonial construction; its early chapters on Colin Mackenzie's collection and native assistants in the Deccan and the south are the relevant ones here. Contested but unavoidable.

  • Matthew H. Edney, Mapping an Empire: The Geographical Construction of British India, 1765–1843 (University of Chicago Press, 1997)

    A history of the Company's surveys, including Mackenzie's Mysore survey and Lambton's Great Trigonometrical Survey, which began at Madras in 1802 and crossed the Deccan. The standard work on the mapping of the peninsula.

  • Michael H. Fisher, Indirect Rule in India: Residents and the Residency System, 1764–1858 (Oxford University Press, 1991)

    The best broad study of the Residency system and the emergence of a specialised British 'political' service. Hyderabad is central to the larger problem even when the book ranges across India.

  • Robert Eric Frykenberg, Guntur District 1788–1848: A History of Local Influence and Central Authority in South India (Clarendon Press, Oxford, 1965)

    A district study of the Northern Circars showing how local revenue officers and landholders captured the Company's administration. An early and influential demonstration of the limits of colonial control in the Telugu country.

  • Ravinder Kumar, Western India in the Nineteenth Century: A Study in the Social History of Maharashtra (Routledge and Kegan Paul, London, 1968)

    A study of the Bombay Deccan after 1818, on the ryotwari settlement, the village, the moneylender and the decline of the Maratha rural elite. The standard social history of the settlement period.

  • Burton Stein, Thomas Munro: The Origins of the Colonial State and His Vision of Empire (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 1989)

    A biography of Munro as administrator of the Ceded Districts and governor of Madras, and of the ryotwari settlement he imposed on the southern Deccan. The standard modern study.

  • Phillip B. Wagoner, Precolonial Intellectuals and the Production of Colonial Knowledge, Comparative Studies in Society and History 45 (4), pp. 783–814 (Cambridge University Press, 2003)

    An article on the Telugu and Marathi scholars who worked for Colin Mackenzie, arguing that they shaped his collection from their own historical traditions rather than serving as passive informants. The best short answer to Dirks on the Mackenzie archive.

The Settlement, 1831–1875

  • Victoria, Queen in Council, Proclamation by the Queen in Council, to the Princes, Chiefs, and People of India (1 November 1858) (Allahabad, 1858)

    The charter of Crown rule after 1857 – it promises to respect the rights, dignity and honour of native princes as our own and disclaims any further extension of territory, formally closing the era of lapse that had swallowed Satara. Read against the Berar assignment and the Mysore question, its guarantees were tested almost at once.

  • Alfred T. Etheridge, Narrative of the Bombay Inam Commission and Supplementary Settlements (Selections from the Records of the Bombay Government, n.s. CXXXII, Bombay, 1874)

    The Commission's own account of its work from 1852 – the scrutiny of sanads and village records by which tens of thousands of inam and saranjam claims were confirmed, reduced or resumed. Indispensable for the scale of the enquiry and the legal reasoning it deployed, and the ground for the grievance that fed into 1857 and beyond.

  • Government of Bombay, The Bombay Hereditary Offices Act, 1874 (Bombay Act III of 1874) – the Watan Act (Bombay Legislative Council, 1874)

    The statute that codified the hereditary district and village offices – patil, kulkarni, deshmukh – and their watan lands as service tenures under the Collector's control, restricting alienation and prescribing commutation. It remained the legal frame for the watandars of the Deccan until the post-Independence abolition acts.

  • Bombay Presidency (Deccan Riots Commission), Report of the Committee on the Riots in Poona and Ahmednagar, 1875, with Appendices (Government Central Press, Bombay, 1876)

    The official enquiry into the attacks on moneylenders at Supa, Kedgaon and the surrounding villages in May 1875 – its evidence on debt, civil-court decrees and land transfer made the Deccan ryot a fixture of Indian economic debate. Its diagnosis led directly to the Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act of 1879.

  • Thomas R. Metcalf, Ideologies of the Raj (The New Cambridge History of India, III.4) (Cambridge University Press, 1994)

    A broader nineteenth-century synthesis useful for situating western Indian institutional change within changing British ideas of rule.

  • Barbara N. Ramusack, The Indian Princes and their States (The New Cambridge History of India, III.6) (Cambridge University Press, 2003)

    Later and broader than the collection's core period, but useful for locating Hyderabad and Mysore within the longer history of indirect rule, subordinate alliance and princely sovereignty.

  • M. G. Ranade, Rise of the Maráthá Power (Punalekar & Co., Girgaum, Bombay, 1900)

    Ranade's reading of Shivaji's state as a national and reforming movement – the Deccan past rewritten by the leading mind of the Sarvajanik Sabha generation, and the text through which the settlement era remembered the kingdom it had replaced.

  • Kenneth Ballhatchet, Social Policy and Social Change in Western India 1817–1830 (Oxford University Press, London, 1957)

    The classic study of Elphinstone's settlement of the conquered Peshwa territories – the decision to keep Maratha institutions, the panchayat, the watandars and the jagirdars, and the first arguments over survey and assessment that Pringle inherited. Sets the terms on which everything after 1831 was a revision.

  • Neil Charlesworth, The Myth of the Deccan Riots of 1875 (Modern Asian Studies, vol. 6, no. 4, pp. 401–421, 1972)

    A sceptical re-reading of the riots – a handful of villages, little bloodshed, and far less transfer of land to moneylenders than the Commission assumed. The starting point for every later argument about what 1875 did and did not show.

  • Neil Charlesworth, Peasants and Imperial Rule: Agriculture and Agrarian Society in the Bombay Presidency 1850–1935 (Cambridge University Press, 1985)

    The wider synthesis – the ryotwari survey settlement at work, the village of 1850, revision settlements, credit, famine and the peasant in politics. Particularly useful on how the Wingate–Goldsmid system was administered and revised after 1868.

  • Ian Copland, The British Raj and the Indian Princes: Paramountcy in Western India, 1857–1930 (Orient Longman, Bombay, 1982)

    How paramountcy was practised after 1858 in the Bombay Presidency's princely states and jagirs, from the Political Agent's office outward. Begins where this collection ends, but its account of residency, tribute, succession and lapse is the frame for the jagirdar and Satara entries.

  • Sumit Guha, The Agrarian Economy of the Bombay Deccan 1818–1941 (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 1985)

    A quantitative economic history of the Deccan districts from the conquest – prices, population, cultivation and the burden of Pringle's over-assessment against the relief of the Wingate settlements. The essential corrective to arguments built on administrative rhetoric alone.

  • Björn Hettne, The Political Economy of Indirect Rule: Mysore 1881–1947 (Curzon Press, London and Malmö, 1978)

    Begins with the rendition of 1881 – the return of Mysore to its Wodeyar ruler after fifty years of Commission rule – and the instrument of transfer that defined what a model native state owed the paramount power. The natural comparison for Satara's lapse and Berar's assignment.

  • Ravinder Kumar, Western India in the Nineteenth Century: A Study in the Social History of Maharashtra (Routledge & Kegan Paul, London, 1968)

    The foundational social history of the period – Pringle's failed survey, Wingate's and Goldsmid's joint report of 1847, the village community under the new revenue rules, and the riots of 1875 as the outcome of a changed relation between cultivator and vani. Still the framework most later work argues with.

  • Rosalind O'Hanlon, Caste, Conflict and Ideology: Mahatma Jotirao Phule and Low Caste Protest in Nineteenth-Century Western India (Cambridge University Press, 1985)

    Phule and the Satyashodhak Samaj of 1873 set against the Brahman-led public life of Poona – the other face of Ranade's generation. Shows how the inam enquiry, the watan question and the cultivator's grievance were read from below.

  • Eric Stokes, The English Utilitarians and India (Clarendon Press, Oxford, 1959)

    The intellectual history behind the revenue survey – Ricardian rent theory, Mill at India House and the ryotwari settlement as an instrument of policy. Explains why Pringle measured net produce and why Wingate abandoned the attempt.

  • Richard P. Tucker, Ranade and the Roots of Indian Nationalism (University of Chicago Press, 1972)

    The standard biography of Ranade as judge, economist and founder of the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, placing his agrarian and social reform writings in the Deccan of the 1870s – the generation that turned the grievances of settlement into a public politics.

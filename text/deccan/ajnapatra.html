The Ajnapatra

1715 · Document · Panhala, Maharashtra

A Marathi manual of statecraft written for the young Kolhapur king by Ramchandra Pant Amatya, one of Shivaji’s surviving ministers.

The Ajnapatra (‘royal edict’) is a Marathi treatise on government of about seven thousand words, dated 19 November 1715 and issued in the name of Sambhaji II of Kolhapur, Tarabai’s rival and Rajaram’s son by Rajasabai. Its author was Ramchandra Nilkanth, the Amatya or finance minister, who had served Shivaji and held the western Deccan for Rajaram during the Jinji years. Most scholars accept his authorship, though some have argued for a secretary writing under his direction.

The first part recalls the achievements of Shivaji and his sons. The second sets out rules: how a king should conduct himself and choose ministers; how the watan-holders, the hereditary deshmukhs and patils, are to be kept in their place; how forts should be provisioned, garrisoned and commanded, since ‘forts are the essence of the kingdom’; how revenue should be assessed and collected without ruining the cultivator; and how to deal with foreign merchants. The passage on Europeans is much quoted. The Amatya advises that the ‘hat-wearers’ – the Portuguese, English, Dutch and French – are not like other traders, since they serve kings and want territory, and should be allowed factories only where they can be watched and never a foothold on the coast or a fort.

The text circulated in Modi-script manuscripts and was printed in the nineteenth century; English translations exist by S. V. Puntambekar (1929) and in Jadunath Sarkar’s House of Shivaji. Its importance is that it is the only extended statement of Maratha political theory from inside the ruling group, written by a man who had practised it. It describes a kingdom of forts and watans, not the cavalry state of chauth and sanads that Balaji Vishwanath was building in the same years at Satara; in that sense it is a conservative document, the politics of Shivaji’s generation set down as the Peshwas were leaving them behind.

In the story

The Ajnapatra states how one Maratha faction understood sovereignty: a king secured by forts, served by appointed officers, and wary of hereditary right and of Europeans alike. Within a decade the Satara Peshwas were building something different, a state of revenue shares and saranjam grants that rewarded exactly the hereditary chiefs the Amatya distrusted. The document lets the reader see the road not taken.

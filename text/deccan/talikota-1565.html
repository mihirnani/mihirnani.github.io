Talikota, 1565

January 1565 · Battle · Rakkasagi-Tangadagi, near Talikota, Karnataka

A coalition of the sultanates defeated Vijayanagara’s army near the Krishna on 23 January 1565, killed Rama Raya and sacked the capital; the empire survived in the south for another century.

On 23 January 1565 – 26 January in some accounts – the combined armies of Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda, with contingents from Bidar, met the army of Vijayanagara under Rama Raya on the plain south of the Krishna between the villages of Rakkasagi and Tangadagi. The battle is conventionally named after Talikota, the town where the allies had camped; Indian scholarship often prefers Rakkasa-Tangadi, and some Kannada sources place the field at Bannihatti. The date is given in the Persian chronicles and is generally accepted.

The allies had married into one another’s houses in 1564 and agreed on a joint campaign; Hussain Nizam Shah of Ahmadnagar, who had most cause for revenge, held the centre. Vijayanagara’s army was larger but its artillery weaker. Rama Raya, an old man, commanded from a litter. The turning point, as Firishta tells it, was a charge by the Ahmadnagar guns and cavalry that broke the centre and a defection by two Muslim commanders in Vijayanagara service; the defection is reported only by later sources and is doubted. Rama Raya was captured and beheaded on the spot, by Hussain’s order or his own hand. His brother Venkatadri was lost; Tirumala escaped to the capital, loaded the treasury onto elephants and withdrew south.

The city was then occupied and plundered for months. The damage was concentrated on the royal centre and on images associated with kingship; the Virupaksha temple continued in worship, and archaeology finds the suburbs decaying rather than burned. The allies did not annex the empire. They returned north with their loot and resumed their own quarrels within a year.

The empire continued under the Aravidu line at Penukonda, Chandragiri and Vellore until the 1640s, but the capital was never reoccupied. The balance that had held the sultanates in check was gone, and the nayakas of the south began to act as kings.

In the story

Talikota closes the first period and the collection treats it with some care, because it has been made to carry more than the evidence allows. It was a battle between states that had fought alongside each other the decade before, won by an alliance formed against one man’s diplomacy, and followed by the victors going home. What changed was the distribution of sovereignty: the great southern layer lost its centre, and the nayakas, Mysore and the sultanates each took a portion. That redistribution, rather than any civilisational rupture, is what the later periods inherit.

The first Anglo-Mysore war

1767–1769 · Treaty · Chennai (Madras), Tamil Nadu

Haidar Ali fought the Company and its allies to a standstill and dictated peace at the gates of Madras in 1769.

The first of four wars between Mysore and the English East India Company ran from 1767 to 1769 and ended with the Treaty of Madras. The treaty date is given as 29 March or 4 April 1769 in different sources. Haidar Ali signed it with a Mysore cavalry force encamped outside the Company’s capital on the Coromandel coast.

The war grew out of a triple alliance of 1766 between the Company, the Nizam of Hyderabad and the Marathas against Haidar, whose seizure of Mysore and the Kanara coast alarmed all three. The Marathas were bought off; the Nizam changed sides and joined Haidar in 1767 after a quarrel over subsidies, then changed back. Colonel Joseph Smith beat the combined Mysore and Hyderabad army at Tiruvannamalai in September 1767, but Haidar’s light horse could not be brought to a decisive battle, and a Company thrust into the Mysore uplands in 1768 ran out of supplies. In March 1769 Haidar split his army, left the Company’s columns behind, and rode some 130 miles in three days to appear before Madras. The Council, with no field force between him and the city, negotiated.

The terms were a mutual restoration of conquests and a promise of mutual aid if either party were attacked. The Company had gained nothing and had been shown that it could not protect the Carnatic, the territory of its client the Nawab of Arcot, against Mysore horse.

The defensive clause mattered most. When the Marathas invaded Mysore in 1770–72, Haidar asked Madras for the help the treaty promised and was refused. He drew the conclusion that the Company’s word was conditional, and the grievance shaped his decision to join the Marathas and the Nizam against it in 1780. The war also established Mysore, a state barely two decades old in its new form, as one of the three Deccan powers with which the Company had to reckon.

In the story

The war is the first direct collision between the Company and a Deccan state, and the Company lost. The shifting alliance of 1766–69, in which the Nizam changed sides twice and the Marathas took a subsidy to stay home, is the layered Deccan in working order: four powers, none dominant, each treating with the others. The treaty’s unkept defence clause is the seed of the next war, and a reminder that for another thirty years the Company was a participant in Deccan politics rather than its arbiter.

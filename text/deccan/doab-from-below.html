The doab from below

c. 1350–1565 · Place · Maski, Raichur district, Karnataka

The wedge between the Krishna and the Tungabhadra is the oldest ground on the plateau, granite and gneiss with a gold belt through it; the two rivers that bound it were the only lines the rock offered, which is why two centuries of states fought over the wedge and none could hold it.

The Raichur doab is usually explained by its rivers. Explain it instead by what lies beneath, and the frontier becomes a fact of geology before it is a fact of politics. The doab sits on the Dharwar craton – the grey Peninsular Gneiss and the granites that welded it, with two Archaean greenstone belts threaded through it, one of which carries the gold of Hutti, worked in antiquity and mined today. Along its northern rim the gneiss dips under the limestones of the Bhima basin, and a few miles beyond, at Bijapur and Gulbarga, the Deccan Traps begin. The doab is therefore the place where the basalt country ends and the granite country starts: deep black soil on the basalt and the limestone to the north, black and red soils patched across the granite of the doab itself. Vijayanagara was a granite kingdom and the Bahmanis a basalt one, and the doab was the seam.

Granite country has no natural frontiers except its rivers. The plateau here is a low, open surface of boulder hills and thin, patchy soil, crossable in any direction, and the only lines that could not be argued with were the Krishna, running east along the trap edge, and the Tungabhadra, running north-east off the craton. Every state that reached the doab claimed to the far river and could hold only to the near one, because the wedge between had nothing to defend it but the two forts on its granite hills, Raichur and Mudgal, and the crossings at Raichur’s fords were what the forts were for. Maski, in the middle of the doab, carries the minor rock edict in which Ashoka is named by name – the earliest sovereign claim on the wedge, cut into the same granite – and Raichur’s fort walls carry inscriptions of the Kakatiyas who held it in 1294 and of the Vijayanagara kings and sultans who took it in turn. The doab was fought for from 1362 to 1520 and changed hands repeatedly without ever changing shape.

The rock also explains the doab’s value. It was not the richest land on either side – the black soil to the north and the irrigated valleys to the south were both better – but it was the crossing, and the crossing controlled the movement of horses, armies and grain between the two Deccans. When Talikota removed one of the two, the frontier vanished in a year: no one needed to hold a seam between two countries that had become one. The rivers went on flowing where they had, and in the twentieth century the Tungabhadra dam turned the granite wedge into the most irrigated ground in the region, which is the doab’s final joke on its history.

In the story

The doab is the collection’s clearest case of a frontier the ground supplied and the states merely occupied: two rivers on a granite floor, a seam between basalt and gneiss, and nothing between them worth more than the crossing.

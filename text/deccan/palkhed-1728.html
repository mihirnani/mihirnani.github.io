Palkhed, 1728

28 February 1728 · Battle · Palkhed, near Vaijapur, Maharashtra

Bajirao’s cavalry campaign trapped the Nizam’s army without water and forced him to concede Maratha revenue rights across the Deccan.

In 1727 Nizam-ul-Mulk stopped paying the chauth, backed the Kolhapur Chhatrapati Sambhaji II against Shahu, and invaded the Maratha home country with a large army and a train of artillery. Bajirao, Peshwa since 1720, declined to meet him. He took his horse north into Khandesh, sacked Jalna and Sindkhed, and then moved through Gujarat and back, covering ground at a pace the Nizam’s column could not match. When the Nizam turned to threaten Pune, Bajirao turned too, and at the end of February 1728 caught the Mughal army near the village of Palkhed, between Aurangabad and Nashik, cut it off from its supplies and from water, and held it there.

There was little fighting. The Nizam negotiated, and on 6 March 1728 at Mungi Shevgaon on the Godavari he agreed to recognise Shahu as sole Chhatrapati, to restore the Maratha revenue collectors he had expelled, to abandon his support for Sambhaji II, and to pay the chauth and sardeshmukhi due. Bajirao had won a campaign against a larger, better-armed force by refusing every engagement it wanted.

Palkhed is the model case of eighteenth-century Maratha warfare, and has been treated as such since Grant Duff; Field Marshal Montgomery’s History of Warfare (1968) called it a masterpiece of strategic mobility. Its method was cavalry with a spare horse for every two men, no baggage, no guns, and a supply system that consisted of the enemy’s countryside. It was not a method for holding territory, and the Marathas never took Hyderabad or displaced the Nizam. What it secured was the revenue settlement of 1719 in practice: after 1728 the Nizam’s Deccan was a province two states taxed. The same campaign also settled the Maratha succession, since Kolhapur never again found an outside sponsor.

In the story

Palkhed shows how a layered settlement was enforced. The Peshwa did not conquer the Nizam’s provinces; he made it too expensive to withhold their revenue. Sovereignty in the Deccan after 1728 was divided by function rather than by frontier – the Nizam governed, the Marathas collected – and this kind of partition, invisible on a map, is what later European cartographers and Company officials found so hard to draw.

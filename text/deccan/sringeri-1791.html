Sringeri, 1791

April 1791 · Event · Sringeri, Karnataka

In April 1791 Maratha irregulars sacked the Sringeri matha; the Swami turned to Tipu Sultan, whose letters ordering its restoration, found in 1916, are the best-documented act of his religious policy.

Sringeri, on the Tunga in the Malnad, was the oldest of the mathas that trace themselves to Shankara, and its Jagadguru was the most eminent Hindu prelate in Mysore. In April 1791, in the second year of the Third Anglo-Mysore War, the Maratha army under Parashuram Bhau Patwardhan was moving through the Malnad as the Company’s ally, and a body of the irregulars attached to it – pindaris and Lamanis, the camp’s unpaid followers, with a Patwardhan detachment under Raghunath Rao of Kurundwad – fell on the matha. They plundered its treasury and granaries, killed a number of Brahmins and others, carried off an elephant and the ornaments of the shrine, and threw down the image of Sharada. The Swami, Sacchidananda Bharati, fled. The Marathas’ own letters, printed by Khare, record the Peshwa’s displeasure and Nana Phadnavis’s orders for restitution, which followed slowly and in part.

The Swami wrote to Tipu. Some thirty of Tipu’s replies, in Kannada, with the orders they carried to his officers, were found in the matha’s records and published by the Mysore Archaeological Department in 1916. They express the sultan’s outrage in the language of dharma – people who had sinned against so holy a place were sure to suffer for it in this Kali age – direct the Asaf of Bednur to supply money and grain for the re-consecration of the image, ask the Swami to perform rites for the prosperity of the state, and continue in the same tone of respect to the end of the reign. Earlier grants by Haidar and Tipu to the matha are on record, and so are Tipu’s gifts to the temples of Srirangapatna, Melkote and Nanjangud.

What the letters prove is narrower than either side of the modern argument wants. They do not cancel the deportations from Coorg and Malabar, or the destruction recorded in Company and Nair accounts; they show a ruler at war treating the great institution of his Hindu subjects as a source of legitimacy and of prayer for victory – kingship as an idiom to be mastered, as Brittlebank reads it, rather than a creed to be imposed, of a piece with his regulations and his embassies. The raid, for its part, was the work of the Deccan’s plural military market: the army that sacked Sringeri was the ally of the Company that would storm Srirangapatna, and its irregulars answered to no one.

Domingos Paes describes Vijayanagara

c. 1520–1522 · Document · Hampi, Karnataka

A Portuguese merchant’s eyewitness account of Krishnadevaraya’s capital, written about 1520–22 and paired with Fernão Nunes’s chronicle of the 1530s, is the fullest outside description of Vijayanagara.

Domingos Paes was a Portuguese trader who travelled from Goa to Vijayanagara in the reign of Krishnadevaraya and wrote an account of what he saw, probably in 1520–22. Fernão Nunes, a horse dealer who spent some years in the city, wrote a longer chronicle of the kingdom’s history in about 1535–37. Both texts survive in a single Portuguese manuscript in Paris, apparently copied for the historian João de Barros, and were first printed in 1897; Robert Sewell translated them into English in A Forgotten Empire (1900), which made them the foundation of modern Vijayanagara studies.

Paes writes as an observer, not a historian. He describes the road in from the coast, the walls and gates, the markets, the palace enclosures and the temples, and judges the city to be as large as Rome and very beautiful to look at. He gives a portrait of the king, of medium height, fair, marked by smallpox, exercising every morning with weights and a wooden sword. The centrepiece is his description of the Mahanavami festival, nine days of processions, wrestling, dancing women and the review of the army, in which the nayakas paraded their troops and presented their tribute to the king.

Nunes supplies what Paes lacks: a narrative of the dynasties, the Raichur campaign of 1520, the horse trade, and the structure of the state, in which the captains held their lands from the king and owed him revenue and soldiers, and could be dismissed.

Together they gave later historians a picture of the capital at its height, only a few decades before 1565, and a view of its political structure from the outside. They also gave the Deccan one of its first European gazes, which is why they sit beside a collection of maps.

In the story

The European Gaze on India is the companion collection, and Paes and Nunes are its earliest prose equivalent: foreigners describing a Deccan state in the terms they had. What they record is telling for the throughline. Nunes sees a king whose captains hold land at his pleasure and parade their contingents each autumn, a sovereignty that is visibly assembled from the loyalties of subordinates. The Mahanavami review was the annual staging of that layered order.

The Battle of Raichur

19 May 1520 · Battle · Raichur, Karnataka

Krishnadevaraya defeated Ismail Adil Shah of Bijapur outside Raichur in May 1520 and took the fort, in a campaign that marks the first significant appearance of cannon and the earliest known use of matchlocks in South Asian warfare.

In 1520 Krishnadevaraya marched on Raichur, the fortress that commanded the doab between the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers, a tract contested between Vijayanagara and the sultanates since the fourteenth century. Ismail Adil Shah of Bijapur came south to relieve it. The armies met on 19 May 1520 near the Krishna. The Bijapur guns at first drove back the Vijayanagara line, but a counter-attack carried the sultan’s camp and he fled across the river. The siege of the fort then resumed; a party of Portuguese under Cristóvão de Figueiredo used their matchlocks to clear the walls, and Raichur surrendered.

Richard Eaton has shown that this campaign marks the first significant appearance of cannon and the earliest known use of matchlock firearms in South Asian warfare. The technology had come to the Deccan by sea: Ottoman gunners and gunsmiths who had fought the Portuguese off the Konkan coast found service at Bijapur, while the Portuguese viceroy supplied Vijayanagara with horses and, on this occasion, with men. Bijapur had the better guns and lost anyway, because cavalry still decided the day in 1520.

The principal source is Fernão Nunes, who wrote in the 1530s and reports the terms Krishnadevaraya set for peace: the sultan must come in person and kiss his foot. Ismail did not come. The demand was remembered in Bijapur, and Eaton reads the bitterness it left as one of the reasons the sultanates could later be brought to combine against the Raya.

Raichur returned the doab to Vijayanagara for a generation. It also showed every ruler in the Deccan what firearms could do, a lesson the sultanates took more seriously than the Rayas, with consequences in 1565.

In the story

Raichur is the collection’s first battle and its first demonstration that the two Deccans were not sealed from one another. Portuguese gunners served the Raya; Turkish gunners served the sultan; the same horse dealers supplied both. Sovereignty was contested with borrowed tools across a frontier that the men fighting crossed freely. The battle also begins the long arc from cavalry to gunpowder that ends, in this timeline, with the Company’s artillery at Seringapatam and Assaye.

Nizam-ul-Mulk establishes the Asaf Jahi state

11 October 1724 · Event · Shakarkheda, Buldhana district, Maharashtra

Victory at Shakarkheda made the Mughal viceroy of the Deccan an autonomous ruler; the Asaf Jahi state dates from it.

Mir Qamar-ud-din Khan, titled Nizam-ul-Mulk (1671–1748), was the son and grandson of Mughal officers who had served Aurangzeb in the Deccan. He was viceroy of the Deccan in 1713–15 and again from 1720, and from 1722 to 1724 vizier of the empire under Muhammad Shah. Finding reform at Delhi impossible, he returned south without leave in 1724. The emperor ordered Mubariz Khan, governor of Hyderabad, to stop him. On 11 October 1724 at Shakarkheda in Berar, Nizam-ul-Mulk defeated and killed Mubariz Khan, sent his head to Delhi, and was in 1725 confirmed as viceroy of the six subahs with the title Asaf Jah.

He never declared independence. He struck coin and read the khutba in the emperor’s name, took imperial orders when they suited him and ignored them when they did not, and went north in 1737 to fight Bajirao on the emperor’s behalf. His court was at Aurangabad; Hyderabad became the capital only under his son Nizam Ali Khan in the 1760s. The state he ran was the Mughal Deccan under new management: the same Persian-writing secretariat, the same mansab ranks, the same layered claims of jagirdar, zamindar and deshmukh. Its difficulty, from the start, was the Marathas, whose chauth and sardeshmukhi he recognised in principle and resisted in practice; the campaigns of 1728 and 1738 both went against him.

When he died at Burhanpur on 1 June 1748 his sons fought for the succession, and the French and the English took sides. The Asaf Jahi state nonetheless lasted until 1948, the longest-lived of the successor states and the one that kept closest to its Mughal form. For the rest of this collection it is the Deccan’s Mughal remnant, a polity that claimed the whole south and, from 1798, survived by lending its sovereignty to the Company.

In the story

Hyderabad is the layer of Mughal sovereignty that did not disappear. Nizam-ul-Mulk’s state governed by the emperor’s authority without the emperor, and shared its revenue with Marathas under the emperor’s own grants. It is the best example in the collection of a polity that was neither independent nor subordinate, a position that later allowed the Company to treat it as a sovereign ally while it functioned as a client.

Tipu’s embassies

1786–1798 · Document · Srirangapatna, Karnataka

Missions to Constantinople and Versailles, and the so-called Jacobin club of Srirangapatna, in Tipu’s search for allies beyond India.

Tipu Sultan sent two major embassies abroad. The first left the port of Tadri on the Kanara coast in March 1786, some nine hundred strong, under Ghulam Ali Khan and Nurullah Khan, bound for the Ottoman court. It reached Constantinople in September 1787 and was received by Abdulhamid I in December. It asked for military help against the Company, for recognition of Tipu’s royal title, and for a trading settlement at Basra; the Ottomans, at war with Russia, offered honours and nothing else. The party returned by way of Mecca in 1789.

The second sailed for France in 1787 with three envoys, Muhammad Dervish Khan, Akbar Ali Khan and Muhammad Usman Khan. Louis XVI received them at Versailles in August 1788, Élisabeth Vigée Le Brun painted two of them, and Paris made them a spectacle, but the treasury was empty and the monarchy a year from collapse. No alliance was made. Tipu also wrote to Zaman Shah of Afghanistan and, earlier, Haidar had corresponded with the French in Mauritius.

The French connection revived in 1797, when François Ripaud, a privateer stranded on the Malabar coast, persuaded Tipu that help could be had from the Republic. A small group of French soldiers at Srirangapatna planted a liberty tree and held a meeting in May 1797 whose minutes, published by the Company after 1799, hail Citizen Tipu. Historians, Jean Boutier among them, treat the Company’s account of a Jacobin club with suspicion: the documents may be embroidered, and the French at Srirangapatna were mercenaries swearing loyalty to the sultan. Ripaud did carry Tipu’s appeal to Mauritius, where the governor Malartic printed it in 1798. That proclamation, reaching Calcutta as Bonaparte landed in Egypt, gave Wellesley his pretext for war.

None of the embassies produced a soldier or a ship. What they produced was a paper trail, and the Company made that trail the justification for the destruction of the state that had written it.

In the story

The embassies show a Deccan ruler treating sovereignty as something exercised in a world of states, reaching past the Mughal emperor and the Company to the Ottoman caliph and the French king for recognition and alliance. That is new for the Deccan. It also shows the danger of the attempt: in a region where the Company was becoming the arbiter of who might deal with whom, correspondence with a European rival could itself be made a casus belli. The Salbai clause excluding other Europeans had become a principle.

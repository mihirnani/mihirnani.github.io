Black soil and the bonds

1847–1875 · Place · Sholapur, Maharashtra

The Traps weather into a black clay that holds water and grows cotton; the survey put a price on it, the American war put a market under it, and the debt that followed the market is what the Riots Commission found in the moneylenders’ bonds.

The Deccan’s history in the last period of this collection was written on one soil. Where the Traps have weathered in a dry climate they leave regur – the black cotton soil of the companion collection – a deep, cracking clay that swells in the rains, holds moisture through the dry months and will carry a crop with no more water than falls on it. It floors Khandesh, Berar, the Nagpur country, Marathwada and the southern Maratha districts from Sholapur to Bijapur, and it spills south onto the limestones of the Bhima. It is the soil of cotton, and cotton is why the plateau’s villages had a cash crop before they had a cash economy.

The Company measured it first. The revenue survey that Goldsmid and Wingate began in 1835 and codified in the Joint Report of 1847 was, before it was anything else, a survey of soil: every field was measured and its soil classed by colour and depth, black above red above the thin ‘barad’ of the uplands, and the assessment was fixed on the class for thirty years. The old regimes had taxed a village’s share of the harvest; the new one taxed a field’s share of the geology, in cash, on a date. Then the American Civil War closed the Lancashire mills’ supply in 1861 and Bombay’s buyers came up the ghats for whatever the black soil would grow. Berar, assigned to the Company in 1853 and reached by rail in the same decade, became the cotton province of India; the Deccan’s older varieties and the American seed that Dharwar had grown since the 1840s went out through Bombay at prices no one had seen. Land was ploughed that had never been, credit followed the price into every taluka, and for four years the regur was the most valuable ground in the peninsula.

The tide went out in 1865. Prices fell by half; the thirty-year settlements of the 1830s came up for revision in the same years, and the revised assessments of Indapur and its neighbours were raised; the seasons of the early 1870s were poor. The debts contracted in the boom had been secured, under the new courts, on the land itself, and the moneylender who had lent against a cotton price now held bonds against fields. The Riots of May and June 1875 broke out not in the deep black soil of Berar but on its thin margin – Bhimthadi, Indapur, Supa, Parner, the talukas of Poona and Ahmednagar where the regur gives way to lighter ground and a cash assessment on a fixed date is hardest to meet. The cultivators burned the bonds, not the moneylenders; they wanted the paper.

What the Commission of 1876 described, without quite saying so, was a chain that ran from the rock to the register. The Traps made the soil; the soil grew the cotton; the survey classed the soil and fixed the demand; the war priced the cotton; the courts made the price into debt; and the debt, when the price fell, became the bonds in the moneylender’s house. Every link in it is an entry in one of this site’s two collections, and none of them explains the riots alone.

In the story

This is where the plateau’s two histories meet on one field. The audit of the last period – the survey, the Inam Commission, the Watan Act – was in the end a reckoning of the soil: who held which class of it, what it owed, and to whom. The Riots are the black soil answering.

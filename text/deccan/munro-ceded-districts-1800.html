Munro and the Ceded Districts

1800–1807 · Institution · Bellary, Karnataka

The districts the Nizam surrendered to the Company in 1800 as the price of its protection, and the collector who settled them field by field with the cultivator himself: the ryotwari system that Madras adopted and that Bombay argued with for fifty years.

By the treaty of 12 October 1800 the Nizam gave up to the Company the territory he had received in the partitions of Mysore in 1792 and 1799 – the country between the Tungabhadra and the Penner that became the Ceded Districts of Bellary and Cuddapah – in return for an enlarged subsidiary force he would no longer have to pay for. It was a raw acquisition: poligar chiefs in their forts, a revenue system disordered by Haidar, Tipu and the Nizam’s farmers in turn, and no Company officer who had governed there. Thomas Munro, a Scottish officer who had spent seven years under Alexander Read settling the Baramahal after 1792, arrived in October 1800 as principal collector, with four assistants and a country of about 27,000 square miles. He reduced the poligars in his first two years, some by pension and several by force, and then did what he had begun to do in the Baramahal: he settled the revenue with each cultivator directly, on a field-by-field survey and assessment, with no intermediary between the ryot and the state.

Ryotwari, as the system came to be called, was a theory of Indian society as much as a method of collection. Munro held that the village and its headman were the ancient institutions of the country, that the zamindars of Bengal and the revenue farmers of the sultanates were parasites upon them, and that the state should deal with the peasant proprietor, assess him moderately and for a fixed term, and leave the village to run itself. His reports from the Ceded Districts, written in the plain and confident prose that made him the most quoted of Company administrators, went to Madras and to London and into the Fifth Report of 1812. Madras adopted the system for the whole presidency in the 1820s, when Munro returned as governor; he died of cholera on tour at Pattikonda, in the Ceded Districts, in July 1827.

Bombay took the argument and not the method. Elphinstone read Munro’s reports and shared his respect for the village, but the Deccan the Company took in 1818 was full of hereditary rights – watans, inams, saranjams – for which Munro’s theory had no place. The survey that Pringle began in the 1820s tried to apply a Ricardian version of the ryotwari assessment and failed; the settlement Goldsmid and Wingate made from 1835 was ryotwari in principle and something more complicated in practice. The Ceded Districts are where the paramount power first tried to reach past every intermediate layer to the man who held the plough; the Bombay Deccan is where it discovered how many layers there were.

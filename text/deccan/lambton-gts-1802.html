Lambton and the Great Trigonometrical Survey

10 April 1802 · Institution · St Thomas Mount, Chennai, Tamil Nadu

A baseline measured near Madras began the triangulation that would carry a web of measured angles across the peninsula and, in time, the subcontinent.

On 10 April 1802 William Lambton began measuring a baseline of about seven and a half miles between St Thomas Mount and Perumbauk Hill, south-west of Madras. The measurement, made with a hundred-foot steel chain of Ramsden’s manufacture laid in wooden coffers and corrected for temperature, took until 22 May. From its two ends Lambton observed the angles to distant hills with a great theodolite made by William Cary, and from those triangles he carried a chain of measured positions westward across the peninsula to Mangalore, completed in 1806, and then south and north along the 78th meridian.

Lambton, born around 1753, had come to India in 1796 with the 33rd Foot, Arthur Wellesley’s regiment, and proposed the survey in 1799 after the fall of Srirangapatna. His aim was geodetic as much as geographical: to fix the figure of the earth by measuring an arc of the meridian, and to provide a framework of exactly known points on which the district surveys of Mackenzie and others could be hung. The Madras government agreed in 1800. A second base was measured at Bangalore in 1804 to check the work. Lambton published his methods and results in Asiatic Researches, and his diagram of the peninsular triangulation is shown in the map collection. The enterprise was named the Great Trigonometrical Survey in 1818. Lambton died in the field at Hinganghat, south of Nagpur, in January 1823, and George Everest carried the Great Arc north to the Himalaya.

The survey made a new kind of map possible. Rennell’s Hindoostan of 1782 had been compiled from route surveys and reports; after Lambton, positions in the peninsula could be fixed to within a few feet. Maps of India published in Europe from the 1820s began, slowly, to show the difference.

In the story

The survey is the collection’s hinge to its companion, the maps. Sovereignty in the Deccan had been expressed in grants, titles and the reach of armies; Lambton’s triangles expressed it as measurement. A state that could fix every hill and fort to a coordinate, and tax every field against a surveyed area, was claiming a kind of authority none of its predecessors had possessed. The Great Trigonometrical Survey began in the conquered territory of Mysore, and that is no accident.

Rama Raya

1542–1565 · Person · Hampi, Karnataka

Krishnadevaraya’s son-in-law ruled Vijayanagara as regent for the puppet king Sadasiva from 1542, played the sultanates against one another for twenty years and died at Talikota.

Aliya Rama Raya, ‘Rama Raya the son-in-law’, was a Telugu noble of the Aravidu family who married a daughter of Krishnadevaraya and served him as a commander. After Achyutadevaraya died in 1542, Rama Raya emerged from the succession struggle as regent for Achyuta’s nephew Sadasiva. He kept the young king in confinement and ruled in his name until 1565; his brothers Tirumala and Venkatadri held the chief commands. Whether he ever took the royal title is unclear; the sultanate chronicles treat him as the king.

His method was intervention. The sultanates were at war with one another through the 1540s and 1550s, and Rama Raya hired out Vijayanagara’s army to whichever side paid. He joined Ahmadnagar against Bijapur, then Bijapur against Ahmadnagar; he marched to Golconda’s support and later against it; in 1558, allied with Ali Adil Shah, he sacked Ahmadnagar territory as far as the capital. He recovered the Raichur doab and took the Bahmani city of Kalyana. His campaigns were profitable and, in the short term, successful: no sultanate could ignore Vijayanagara or safely offend it.

The policy wore out. The chroniclers, especially Firishta writing for Bijapur, describe the conduct of Vijayanagara’s troops in Ahmadnagar’s lands and Rama Raya’s own arrogance toward the sultans as the grievances that brought Hussain Nizam Shah, Ali Adil Shah and Ibrahim Qutb Shah together in 1564, sealed by marriages. Modern historians read the coalition less as a religious reaction than as the balance of power reasserting itself against a regent who had exploited it too well.

He led the army to the Krishna in January 1565, was captured in the battle and beheaded. His brother Tirumala took the dynasty south to Penukonda.

In the story

Rama Raya is the figure who shows that the two Deccans were one political system. For twenty years the regent of Vijayanagara was a regular participant in sultanate wars, an ally by turns of each court and an employer of Muslim officers. His fall came when the system he had worked so well turned on him. The collection treats 1565 as the end of one balance of power among several, and his career explains why the balance existed.

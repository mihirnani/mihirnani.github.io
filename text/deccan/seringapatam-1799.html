Seringapatam, 1799

4 May 1799 · Battle · Srirangapatna, Karnataka

The storming of Tipu’s capital by Harris’s army and the Nizam’s contingent, in which Tipu Sultan was killed and the Mysore sultanate ended.

The fortress of Srirangapatna was stormed on the afternoon of 4 May 1799 by the army of General George Harris, and Tipu Sultan was killed in the fighting. The fourth Anglo-Mysore war had lasted about two months.

Wellesley had resolved on war in 1798 on the strength of the Malartic proclamation from Mauritius and Tipu’s correspondence with the French. His letters to Tipu in November 1798 and January 1799 demanded the reception of an envoy and the dismissal of the French; Tipu’s answers were evasive, and Harris marched from Vellore in February with about 4,000 Europeans, 22,000 sepoys and the Nizam’s subsidiary contingent, with a second column under Stuart from Bombay. Tipu fought a delaying action at Malavalli on 27 March and fell back on the island. The siege opened on 5 April. The batteries of the Nizam’s force and the Company opened a practicable breach in the west wall on 2 May.

Baird led the assault across the river at about one in the afternoon. The breach was carried in minutes and the attackers spread along the ramparts. Tipu, fighting at the north face, was shot and killed near the Water Gate as the defenders crowded through; his body was found that evening under a heap of dead. Mysore accounts blame his minister Mir Sadiq for withdrawing men from the breach at the crucial moment; the charge has never been settled. The town was looted that night and the next day until Harris restored order. Prize agents later distributed over a million pounds, and Tipu’s throne, library, arms and the tiger organ were dispersed to London.

Tipu was buried beside his father in the Gumbaz on 5 May with a Company guard of honour. His sons were sent to Vellore and later to Calcutta. The Company, the Nizam and, on paper, the Marathas divided the territory, and the Wodeyar child Krishnaraja III was placed on a reduced Mysore throne under a subsidiary treaty. Seringapatam, in Company usage, became the name of a victory, on medals and in the title of the regiments that carried it.

In the story

The fall of Srirangapatna removed the last Deccan state that had claimed full sovereignty against the Company. Its conduct is as telling as its outcome: the Nizam’s army fought under Company command as a subsidiary force, the Marathas took no part and got nothing, and the settlement was written in Calcutta. The plural Deccan of 1792, in which three powers had divided Mysore as allies, had become one in which a single power decided what Mysore would be.

Madhavrao I

1761–1772 · Person · Theur, Maharashtra

The fourth Peshwa, who took office at sixteen after Panipat and in eleven years rebuilt Maratha power in the Deccan and the north.

Madhavrao Ballal became Peshwa in June 1761, aged sixteen, a few months after the Maratha defeat at Panipat and the death of his father Balaji Bajirao. He governed from Pune until his death from tuberculosis at Theur, near the city, on 18 November 1772. He was twenty-seven.

The reign opened under the regency of his uncle Raghunathrao, and the two were at odds almost at once. Raghunathrao defeated his nephew at Alegaon in November 1762 and took control; the Nizam, Nizam Ali, exploited the quarrel and marched on Pune. Uncle and nephew combined to beat him at Rakshasbhuvan on the Godavari on 10 August 1763. Madhavrao then asserted himself, and after a further breach in 1768 he defeated and confined Raghunathrao at Shaniwar Wada. Against Haidar Ali of Mysore he led or sent campaigns in 1764–65, 1767 and 1770–72, recovering tribute and the districts north of the Tungabhadra that Haidar had taken. In the north he sent Mahadji Scindia and Tukoji Holkar in 1769; they beat the Jats and Rohillas and in 1771 took Delhi, returning the emperor Shah Alam II to his capital under Maratha protection.

Madhavrao was also an administrator. He checked the abuses of revenue farming, supported Ramshastri Prabhune as chief justice, and kept the great houses of the confederacy, Scindia and Holkar in particular, answerable to Pune. His court was frugal by the standards of the time.

His death removed the one figure who could hold the Pune government and its sardars together. His brother Narayanrao was murdered within a year, Raghunathrao’s bid for the office drew in the Company, and the Maratha state passed to a regency of ministers under Nana Phadnavis. Grant Duff, the Company historian of the Marathas, judged that the plains of Panipat were less fatal to the Maratha state than the early death of this prince; later historians have largely agreed.

In the story

Madhavrao’s reign shows that Panipat was not the end of Maratha power in the Deccan. The confederacy recovered, beat the Nizam, pressed Mysore and re-entered Delhi within a decade. It also shows how far that recovery rested on one man’s ability to hold the layered structure of Chhatrapati, Peshwa and sardars together. When he died the layers came apart, and the succession dispute that followed gave the Company its first opening into Maratha politics.

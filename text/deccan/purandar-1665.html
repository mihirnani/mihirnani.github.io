The Treaty of Purandar and Agra

11 June 1665 · Treaty · Purandar, Pune district, Maharashtra

Shivaji surrenders twenty-three forts to Jai Singh, goes to Aurangzeb’s court at Agra, is confined, and escapes – by the best-known account, hidden in a basket of sweets.

On 11 June 1665 Shivaji signed a treaty with Mirza Raja Jai Singh of Amber, Aurangzeb’s commander in the Deccan, outside the fort of Purandar, which Jai Singh had been besieging since March. Shivaji gave up twenty-three of his thirty-five forts and the territory attached to them, keeping twelve with an annual revenue reckoned at a lakh of huns; he agreed to serve the empire with his cavalry, his son Sambhaji being enrolled as a mansabdar of five thousand; and he undertook to pay forty lakhs of huns in instalments in return for the right to collect revenue from specified Bijapuri districts in the Konkan and the Ghats, a clause that in effect licensed him to take from Bijapur what he had lost to the Mughals.

Jai Singh had done what Shaista Khan could not, by occupying the open country systematically and forcing Shivaji to choose between ruin and terms. The treaty was followed by a joint Mughal and Maratha campaign against Bijapur, which failed, and by Jai Singh’s proposal that Shivaji go north to be received by the emperor.

Shivaji reached Agra in May 1666 with Sambhaji and a small retinue. At the audience of 12 May he was placed among the mansabdars of five thousand, far back in the hall, and protested aloud; he was removed and confined under guard. On 17 August he escaped, according to the best-known account by hiding in one of the large baskets of sweets that he had been sending out as gifts, and reached the Deccan some weeks later by a circuitous route. Jai Singh’s son Ram Singh, responsible for the guard, was disgraced.

Purandar was the only time Shivaji accepted a place inside the Mughal order, and Agra was the moment he decided it was not worth having. Within four years he had retaken most of the ceded forts.

In the story

Purandar and Agra test the period’s central question: could the Mughal system absorb a Maratha chief the way it had absorbed Rajput kings? Jai Singh, himself a Rajput, thought so, and the treaty was drafted on that model. The ranking at court, and the escape, showed that the model did not transfer. The collection treats 1665–66 as the point where the Marathas moved from a possible layer within the empire to a rival claim against it.

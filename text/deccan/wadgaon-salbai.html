Wadgaon and Salbai

1779–1782 · Treaty · Vadgaon Maval, Maharashtra

A Company army surrendered to the Marathas in the Western Ghats in 1779; the Treaty of Salbai ended the first Anglo-Maratha war three years later.

The first Anglo-Maratha war began in 1775 when the Bombay government backed Raghunathrao’s claim to be Peshwa against the regency in Pune. Its turning point came on 12–13 January 1779 at Wadgaon, in the Maval country between Bombay and Pune. A Bombay force of about 3,000 fighting men, moving slowly up the Ghats with a vast baggage train, was surrounded by Mahadji Scindia’s horse, cut off from supplies and forced to negotiate. The convention signed at Wadgaon a few days later returned all Company acquisitions since 1773, Salsette included.

Calcutta repudiated the convention at once, on the ground that Bombay had no authority to make it, and Warren Hastings sent Colonel Goddard across the subcontinent from Bengal to carry on the war. Goddard took Ahmedabad and Bassein in 1780; Captain Popham took Gwalior, Scindia’s fortress, in the same year. Meanwhile Haidar Ali’s invasion of the Carnatic in 1780 drew Company resources south and made peace with the Marathas urgent.

Mahadji Scindia mediated. The Treaty of Salbai, near Gwalior, was signed on 17 May 1782 by Scindia and David Anderson for the Company and ratified by Nana Phadnavis for the Pune government the next year. The Company kept Salsette and Broach, recognised the infant Madhavrao II as Peshwa, and agreed to pension Raghunathrao. The Marathas undertook to allow no European settlements other than the Company’s and to recover the Carnatic districts Haidar had taken.

Salbai gave the Company and the Marathas twenty years of peace. It also confirmed Scindia as the broker between Pune, Delhi and Calcutta, a position he used to build the largest army in India. The Company had learnt that it could not take on the confederacy from Bombay alone and would need the Bengal army and a Maratha partner; both lessons were applied in 1803.

In the story

Wadgaon is the clearest evidence in the collection that the Company was not yet paramount: its army surrendered, and its treaty was torn up by its own superiors. Salbai records the working arrangement that followed, a peace in which the Company held one island and one port and left the Deccan to Pune. For the throughline, the treaty’s provision excluding other Europeans is the first time a Deccan state accepted a clause written in the Company’s interest.

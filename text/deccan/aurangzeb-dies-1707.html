Aurangzeb dies at Ahmadnagar

3 March 1707 · Event · Ahmadnagar, Maharashtra

The emperor’s death in his Deccan camp, after twenty-six years in the south, left an empire overextended and a war unwon.

Aurangzeb died in his camp at Ahmadnagar on 3 March 1707, aged eighty-eight, and was buried in an open grave at Khuldabad beside the shrine of the Chishti saint Zainuddin Shirazi, as he had asked. He had come south in 1681 to finish the Deccan and had not seen northern India since. In those years he had annexed Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687), executed Sambhaji (1689), and taken, one after another, nearly every Maratha fort; and he had not ended the war.

The cost was visible to his own officers. Bhimsen Saxena, a Hindu soldier-clerk in the imperial service whose memoir Tarikh-i-Dilkasha is the best eyewitness account, described an army dragging a moving city across a ruined country, forts bought with gold and lost again within months, and Maratha horse collecting chauth in the emperor’s own provinces. Revenue from the new Deccan subahs never matched the expense of holding them, and jagirs assigned there could not be collected; the resulting shortage of paid jagirs, the crisis Satish Chandra later analysed, pressed on the whole mansabdari system. Aurangzeb’s last letters to his sons are despairing in tone. The war of succession that followed, settled at Jajau in June 1707 in favour of Bahadur Shah, began with Azam Shah’s release of Shahu, a decision taken to divide the Marathas and which did so.

The Mughal Deccan did not collapse in 1707. Its six subahs remained on paper, its governors continued to be appointed from Delhi, and one of them, Nizam-ul-Mulk, would turn the viceroyalty into a state of his own. But the emperor had been the empire’s one reliable source of authority in the south, and with him gone the layers beneath – Maratha collectors, Mughal governors, local deshmukhs, the Company at Bombay – began to negotiate with each other directly. The eighteenth century in the Deccan is the history of those negotiations.

In the story

Aurangzeb’s death is the hinge of the collection. For a generation the Mughal empire had tried to be the single sovereign of the Deccan and had come closer than anyone before it. What it left instead was a set of overlapping claims – imperial, Maratha, provincial – none of which could exclude the others. The layering the throughline describes became, after 1707, the normal condition of the south rather than a problem awaiting solution.

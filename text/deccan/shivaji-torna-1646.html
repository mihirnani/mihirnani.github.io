Shivaji takes Torna

1646 · Event · Torna, Pune district, Maharashtra

A sixteen-year-old Shivaji seizes a Bijapuri hill fort above Pune, the first of the strongholds from which a new state would be built.

In 1646 Shivaji Bhonsle, then about sixteen, took possession of Torna, a hill fort some fifty kilometres south-west of Pune, from its Bijapuri garrison. Some accounts put the event in 1647; the Maratha chronicles are not precise, and the date is conventional. He renamed the fort Prachandagad and, the chronicles say, used treasure found there to begin building a new fort on a neighbouring hill, Rajgad, which became his first capital.

Shivaji had grown up at Pune, in the jagir his father Shahji held from Bijapur, under the care of his mother Jijabai and the administrator Dadoji Kondadev. Shahji was far away in Bengaluru. The Pune country had been fought over for forty years, first by Malik Ambar’s raiders and the Mughals, then in the wars that ended Ahmadnagar, and the hill forts of the Sahyadri were held by Bijapuri commanders whose grip was light. Over the following years Shivaji added Kondana, Purandar, Rajgad and others, sometimes by assault and more often by bribery, negotiation or the death of a holder. Bijapur, which had its own troubles, responded in 1648 by arresting Shahji, and Shivaji kept quiet until his father was released.

What was happening is easy to misread. Shivaji was not yet at war with anyone; he was a jagirdar’s son consolidating control of his father’s estate and of the forts that commanded it, as deshmukhs in the hill country had always tried to do. The difference was one of degree and of intent. He built and garrisoned the forts with his own men, administered the villages directly, and did not seek confirmation from Bijapur.

By the time Bijapur took him seriously, in the late 1650s, he held a compact territory in the Western Ghats with its own revenue and army, and the sultanate had to send Afzal Khan to recover it.

In the story

Torna is the smallest event in this period and the one with the longest consequence. A fort changed hands in a country where forts changed hands constantly, but this time the new holder declined to serve the sultan who nominally owned it. The story of the collection is the narrowing of sovereignty, and Shivaji’s forts are a countercurrent: a new claimant inserting itself between the Mughals and the sultanates, in the hills neither of them could hold.

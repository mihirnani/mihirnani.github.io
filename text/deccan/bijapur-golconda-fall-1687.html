Aurangzeb takes Bijapur and Golconda

1686–1687 · Event · Golconda, Hyderabad, Telangana

Bijapur surrenders in September 1686 and Golconda falls by treachery a year later, ending two centuries of Deccan sultanates and bringing the Mughal frontier to the Kaveri.

Aurangzeb came south in 1681 with the imperial court and army, ostensibly in pursuit of his rebel son Akbar, who had taken refuge with Sambhaji, and he never returned north. In April 1685 his forces invested Bijapur, and after fifteen months of siege the young sultan Sikandar Adil Shah surrendered on 12 September 1686; the emperor entered the city, the Adil Shahi state was annexed, and Sikandar lived out his life as a prisoner, dying in 1700. Aurangzeb then turned on Golconda. The siege began in January 1687 and lasted eight months through the monsoon, with famine in the camp and assaults repeatedly beaten off, until on 21 September 1687 a Qutb Shahi officer opened a gate to the Mughals at night. Abul Hasan Qutb Shah, known as Tana Shah, was imprisoned at Daulatabad and died there; his treasury, reputed to be the richest in India, went to the emperor.

Aurangzeb had wanted the sultanates’ wealth and their submission, and he had a religious case against Golconda, whose Shia sultan employed Brahmin ministers, Madanna and Akkanna, and had paid Shivaji for protection. He also wanted to deny the Marathas their sanctuary and their paymasters. The conquest did both and neither. The sultanates’ armies, dismissed rather than absorbed, went into Maratha service, and the territory, stretching to the Kaveri, was larger than the Mughal administration could govern.

The emperor spent the remaining twenty years of his life in the Deccan, besieging Maratha forts one by one, and died at Ahmadnagar in 1707 with the war unfinished.

With Bijapur and Golconda gone, the empire claimed the whole Deccan and held only its towns. The Mughal south, begun at Ahmadnagar in 1600, was complete on the map in 1687, and on the ground was already passing to the Marathas and to the Mughal governors who would shortly make Hyderabad their own.

In the story

1687 is the collection’s first collapse of plurality, and it is instructive that it failed. By removing the sultanates Aurangzeb removed the intermediaries that had absorbed Maratha energy and Mughal demands alike, and he inherited their enemies without their local knowledge. The layers did not disappear; they reformed, as the Maratha state and the Nizam’s. The Company a century later would manage the same consolidation more durably, by keeping the intermediaries and taking their revenues instead.

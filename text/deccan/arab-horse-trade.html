The horse trade

16th century · Institution · Bhatkal, Karnataka

Warhorses shipped from Arabia and the Gulf through Goa, Bhatkal and the Konkan ports were the Deccan’s essential military import, and control of them was a matter of state.

The Deccan does not breed good cavalry horses. Heat, fodder and disease wore them out, and the armies of Vijayanagara and the sultanates alike depended on a yearly supply from Arabia, Persia and the Gulf, shipped from Hormuz and Aden to the western ports. The trade was old; the Persian envoy Abdur Razzaq described it at Vijayanagara in the 1440s, and Fernão Nunes, himself a horse dealer, wrote in the 1530s that the king paid for the animals and replaced them generously, a policy intended to keep the dealers coming to him rather than to Bijapur.

The ports were the prize. Goa had been the chief horse mart under the Bahmanis and Bijapur, which is why Albuquerque took it in 1510 and then decreed that all horse ships must unload there. Vijayanagara, cut off from Goa by Bijapur territory, used Bhatkal and Honavar on the Kanara coast, and treated with the Portuguese to keep the supply open. Prices were high, several hundred pardaos for a fine animal, many times a craftsman’s annual wage, and the customs on horses were among the richest revenues of the Estado da Índia.

With the horses came people: Arab and Persian dealers, Turkish gunners, Abyssinian soldiers bought in the same ports, and the Portuguese themselves. The trade bound the plateau to the Arabian Sea and gave the coastal powers, first the Portuguese and later the Europeans generally, a lever on inland politics that their small numbers would not otherwise have justified.

The dependence lasted as long as cavalry decided battles. The Marathas would later breed and raid for their own horses; the Company would win with infantry and guns. In the sixteenth century the Deccan still bought its power by the shipload.

In the story

The horse trade is the collection’s reminder that sovereignty on the plateau was financed and armed from the sea. Neither Deccan could fight without imports, so each had to deal with merchants and with the Portuguese who taxed them. It is an early and concrete instance of the pattern in which a maritime power with little land gained influence over landed states, a pattern that the Company would complete.

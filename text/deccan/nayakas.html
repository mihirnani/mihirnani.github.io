The nayakas

c. 1529–1565 and after · Institution · Madurai, Tamil Nadu

The military governors Vijayanagara placed over Madurai, Thanjavur, Jinji and Keladi became, after 1565, kings in their own right and the successor states of the southern Deccan.

Vijayanagara governed its southern provinces through nayakas, commanders granted territory in return for troops and tribute. Nunes describes the arrangement in the 1530s: the captains held their lands at the king’s pleasure and brought their contingents to the capital each year. In the early sixteenth century the Rayas installed nayakas over the Tamil country who founded lasting lines. Viswanatha Nayaka was sent to Madurai by Achyutadevaraya in 1529; Sevappa Nayaka received Thanjavur in about 1532; Tubaki Krishnappa Nayaka held Jinji from about 1509, though the dates of these beginnings vary by source. In the Kannada uplands, Chaudappa Nayaka of Keladi, a chief confirmed by Krishnadevaraya in about 1499, began the house that later ruled from Ikkeri and Bednur.

After 1565 the tie to the centre loosened. The Aravidu kings at Penukonda and Chandragiri still received tribute and embassies from the nayakas, and the nayakas used Vijayanagara titles and forms, but they issued their own grants, fought their own wars and built their own capitals. Madurai enlarged the Minakshi temple; Thanjavur under Raghunatha Nayaka became a centre of Telugu letters; Keladi declared itself free of Vijayanagara after the battle and held the Kanara coast against the Portuguese.

The nayaka courts were Telugu-speaking dynasties ruling Tamil and Kannada populations, with Balija merchant-warriors in their service and a court culture that Narayana Rao, Shulman and Subrahmanyam have described in Symbols of Substance (1992). They lasted until the mid-seventeenth century, when Bijapur took Jinji (1649), and into the eighteenth, when Thanjavur passed to the Marathas (1675) and Madurai to the Nawab of Arcot; Keladi fell to Haidar Ali in 1763.

Their emergence meant that the fall of Vijayanagara did not leave a vacuum in the south. It left several middle-sized states, each with a claim to the old empire’s legitimacy and none with its reach.

In the story

The nayakas are the collection’s demonstration that sovereignty in the Deccan was delegated before it was divided. They were governors who became kings without a revolution, by continuing to do what they had done while the centre faded. That mode of succession, in which a subordinate layer inherits the functions of the layer above it, recurs with the Mughal governors who became Nizams and the Peshwas who eclipsed the Chhatrapati. The southern Deccan after 1565 is the first and clearest example.

The Satara raj

1818–1848 · Institution · Satara, Maharashtra

The Company freed Shivaji’s descendant from the Peshwa’s custody and made him a small treaty raja, then deposed his line within a generation.

Pratapsinh Bhonsle, the Chhatrapati of Satara and a direct descendant of Shivaji, had been a prisoner of the Peshwa for most of his life, confined at Satara and at the hill fort of Wasota. In 1818 the Company released him and, at Elphinstone’s suggestion, set him up as raja of a new state carved from the Peshwa’s possessions. The choice was deliberate. The Company could present itself as liberating the legitimate Maratha sovereign from a usurping minister, and in the proclamation of February 1818 Elphinstone invited the Maratha country to see the war in those terms. A treaty signed on 25 September 1819 defined the state: the districts around Satara together with parts of the modern Pune, Sangli, Solapur and Bijapur districts, with James Grant Duff, later historian of the Marathas, as the first Resident.

Pratapsinh governed with some energy, building roads and palaces and supporting schools. But the relationship soured. Accused of intriguing against the Company, he was deposed on 5 September 1839 and sent to Benares, where he died on 14 October 1847. His agent Rango Bapuji Gupte spent years in London pressing his case in Parliament and the Court of Directors without success. His brother Shahaji, called Appasaheb, succeeded him and died without a natural heir in 1848. The Company refused to recognise an adopted son and annexed Satara under what came to be called the doctrine of lapse.

The Satara raj lasted thirty years. It was created to give the conquest of 1818 a Maratha legitimacy, and it was abolished when that legitimacy was no longer required.

In the story

Satara makes visible the way the Company handled the oldest layer of Maratha sovereignty. The Chhatrapati’s authority, from which the Peshwas derived theirs, was revived in 1818 precisely because it was useful for unseating the Peshwa. Once the Company was secure, the same authority became an inconvenience and was dismantled by deposition and lapse. Paramountcy could recognise, restore and extinguish sovereignties as policy required, which is the clearest sign that it stood above them all.

The Treaty of Seringapatam

18 March 1792 · Treaty · Srirangapatna, Karnataka

Cornwallis’s coalition took half of Mysore and two of Tipu’s sons as hostages at the end of the third Anglo-Mysore war.

The Treaty of Seringapatam was signed on 18 March 1792 outside the walls of Tipu’s capital by Lord Cornwallis, as Governor-General, and by the agents of the Nizam and the Peshwa, the Company’s allies in the third Anglo-Mysore war. Tipu had already handed over two of his sons, Abdul Khaliq, aged about ten, and Muiz-ud-din, aged about eight, on 26 February, as security for the terms.

The war had begun when Tipu attacked Travancore, a Company ally, in December 1789. Cornwallis built a triple alliance with Hyderabad and Pune in 1790, took command himself in 1791, and after taking Bengaluru and failing before Srirangapatna for want of supplies, returned in February 1792 with a siege train and the allied contingents. The assault on the outworks on the night of 6 February left the fort exposed, and Tipu negotiated.

He ceded half his territory. The Company took Malabar, Coorg as a dependency, and the Baramahal and Dindigul districts on the eastern side; the Nizam took the country between the Krishna and the Penner with the forts of Cuddapah and Gandikota; the Peshwa took the land up to the Tungabhadra. An indemnity of three crore and thirty lakh rupees was payable in instalments, and the princes were held at Madras until it was paid. They were returned on 29 March 1794. The reception of the hostage princes was painted repeatedly in Britain, by Mather Brown, Robert Home and others, as an image of Cornwallis’s clemency.

The treaty left Mysore an inland state without a coast, ringed by its enemies and carrying a debt. Cornwallis chose not to destroy it, judging that a weakened Mysore balanced the Marathas, and the Company shared the spoils with Indian allies as an equal partner for the last time. Tipu spent the next seven years rebuilding his army and seeking allies abroad, and the Company spent them deciding that the balance was no longer wanted.

In the story

Seringapatam in 1792 is a treaty of the old Deccan order made under the new dispensation. Three powers divided a fourth, as the sultanates had divided Vijayanagara after Talikota, but the Company drew the boundaries and held the hostages. Cornwallis’s decision to preserve Mysore as a counterweight was the last occasion on which the Company behaved as one Deccan power balancing others; its successors took the view that balance itself was the problem.

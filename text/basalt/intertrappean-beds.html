Between the flows

c. 66 Ma · Formation · Takli, Nagpur, Maharashtra

In the pauses between eruptions, lakes and soils formed on the cooling lava; their thin fossil-rich layers – the intertrappean beds – are the Deccan’s diary of the extinction interval.

The Traps did not erupt continuously. Between flows there were pauses – years, centuries, occasionally much longer – in which the lava surface weathered to soil, ponds and lakes gathered in its hollows, and life moved back in. Buried by the next flow, these interludes survive as the intertrappean beds: thin bands of limestone, chert and clay sandwiched in the basalt pile, famous out of all proportion to their thickness. From the classic localities – Takli near Nagpur among the first described – they have yielded fish, frogs, turtles, molluscs, ostracods and a celebrated flora of fruits, seeds and wood, preserved in silica fine enough to keep cell walls.

Their scientific weight comes from position: the beds record ordinary freshwater life carrying on during the eruption interval that spans the end-Cretaceous extinction. Assemblages change upward through the pile – the boundary’s fingerprints inside the volcano – and the beds have supplied much of the Indian evidence in the extinction debate. They also preserve, at several horizons, some of the last dinosaur eggshell and mammal faunas from immediately around the Cretaceous–Palaeogene boundary, a page-turn made in stone.

The early Nagpur collections were made in the 1830s by Company officers – the intertrappeans are where the Deccan’s amateur geology began – and the beds’ silicified wood, weathering out as glittering logs, had been noticed, and occasionally worshipped, long before it was named.

In the story

The intertrappeans are the human-scale windows in an inhuman event: ponds with frogs in them, between floods of lava. They carry the collection’s biology through the Traps period – and they introduce the Company collectors who return, as protagonists, in the final entry.

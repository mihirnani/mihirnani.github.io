The Cuddapah basin

c. 1,900 Ma – later Proterozoic · Formation · Gandikota, Andhra Pradesh

A crescent of Proterozoic sandstones, shales and limestones lying on the craton’s floor in Rayalaseema – quiet rocks that later gave the Deccan its gorges, slabs and diamond-bearing conglomerates.

East of the granite country the basement dips beneath a great crescent of layered rock: the Cuddapah basin, some 44,000 square kilometres of sandstones, shales, limestones and volcanic sills laid down in shallow Proterozoic seas and river plains from about 1,900 million years ago, with the overlying Kurnool group carrying the record into the later Proterozoic – how much later is genuinely debated, for the Kurnool beds have so far resisted direct dating. Nothing in these rocks moved fast. They accumulated a few metres in a million years on a craton that barely stirred, and they remain today almost flat-lying except along their folded eastern rim – among the least deformed very old sedimentary piles on earth.

Their gifts to the later Deccan are practical. The quartzites make the scarps and gorges of Rayalaseema – the Penna’s canyon at Gandikota, the ledges that carry the hill forts of the Deccan timeline’s frontier country; the Kurnool limestones burn to lime and take a polish, quarried from the days of the sultanates to the cement towns of today; the shales split into the slabs and flags of every bazaar floor in the region. And in the basal conglomerates of the Kurnool group lie recycled diamonds, washed out of the craton’s kimberlites and locked in Proterozoic gravel – an early harvest of the pipes, though recent work argues these particular conglomerates did not supply Golconda’s famous stones.

The basin also preserves some of India’s oldest visible life: stromatolites, the layered mounds of microbial mats, dome after dome in the Cuddapah limestones. The Deccan’s first builders were bacteria, and their masonry is still in place.

In the story

The Cuddapah country is the collection’s reminder that ‘the Deccan’ is not all basalt: the south-east runs on Proterozoic sediment, and its gorges, forts, lime and diamonds enter the human timeline repeatedly. The stromatolites, meanwhile, put the first life on the plateau two billion years before the first hearth.

Dinosaurs at the edge of the Traps

c. 70–66 Ma · Formation · Lameta Ghat, Jabalpur, Madhya Pradesh

The Lameta beds along the plateau’s northern rim preserve India’s last dinosaurs – titanosaurs and the predator Rajasaurus, with nesting grounds of fossil eggs – sealed beneath the first lava flows.

Immediately beneath the Traps, along the Narmada line and at scattered windows across the northern Deccan, lie the Lameta beds: pale limestones and sandstones of rivers and floodplains from the last few million years of the Cretaceous. They hold India’s final dinosaur fauna – heavy-bodied titanosaur sauropods, and the horned abelisaurid predator Rajasaurus narmadensis, described in 2003 from bones collected at Rahioli in Gujarat, at the other end of the Narmada country – together with some of the world’s great dinosaur nesting grounds: thousands of cannonball eggs, singly and in clutches, along hundreds of kilometres of the Narmada country. The first of these bones entered science in 1828, when Captain W. H. Sleeman – better remembered for his later campaign against the thugs – dug ‘petrified bones’ from Bara Simla hill in the Jabalpur cantonment.

The beds’ position gives them their force: they are the floor on which the Traps stand. The animals of the Lameta plains lived in sight of the eruptions’ beginning – ash and lava appear interbedded with the uppermost nests – and their world did not survive the end-Cretaceous volcanic and extinction interval. In the debate over what killed the dinosaurs, the Lameta–Trap contact is India’s exhibit: a fauna flourishing, an eruption starting, a silence.

Isolated as it drifted, Cretaceous India has also become a test case for how faunas evolve on a continental raft; the affinities of its dinosaurs, frogs and mammals with Madagascar and South America are still argued over, one more way the plateau’s rocks are read for connection.

In the story

The Lameta beds give the plateau inhabitants on the eve of the eruptions: when the Traps arrive in the next period, the reader knows the world they buried. And the first of these bones were dug up in 1828 by a Company officer, Sleeman, off duty – the earliest appearance of the men with whom the collection closes, reading the rock between their other work.

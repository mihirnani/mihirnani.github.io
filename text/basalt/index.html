Basalt and Laterite

The ground under the Deccan and how it came to be: an old craton, a flood of lava, a laterite crust, and the soils, rivers and stone the plateau’s people have built with.

01

The Basement

c. 3,600–2,500 Ma

The oldest Deccan: grey gneisses, greenstone belts and a final flood of granite weld the southern craton together.

  • c. 3,400–2,500 Ma The Peninsular Gneiss

    The grey banded gneisses that floor the southern peninsula are among the oldest rocks in India; the hill in Bengaluru’s Lalbagh garden is their classic exposure, protected as a national geological monument.

    Formation · The old craton

  • c. 2,900–2,600 Ma The Dharwar greenstones

    Belts of altered lavas and sediments folded into the gneiss country – the remains of Archaean sea floors, and the source of the craton’s iron, manganese and gold.

    Formation · The old craton

  • c. 2,550 Ma; mined to AD 2001 Kolar and the old gold

    Gold-bearing quartz veins in a greenstone belt east of Bengaluru, worked in antiquity, reopened under the Raj, and mined to depths of three kilometres before closing in 2001.

    Place · The old craton

  • c. 2,500 Ma The Closepet Granite

    A four-hundred-kilometre ribbon of pink granite marking the craton’s final welding – and, weathered into domes and boulder piles, the theatrical scenery of the southern Deccan.

    Formation · The old craton

02

The Covered Craton

c. 2,500–250 Ma

Two billion quiet years: sedimentary basins settle on the old floor, kimberlites bring up diamonds, ice comes and goes, and Gondwana’s rivers bury the coal.

  • c. 1,900 Ma – later Proterozoic The Cuddapah basin

    A crescent of Proterozoic sandstones, shales and limestones lying on the craton’s floor in Rayalaseema – quiet rocks that later gave the Deccan its gorges, slabs and diamond-bearing conglomerates.

    Formation · Sedimentary basins

  • Precambrian, repeatedly reactivated The old scar beneath the plateau

    An ancient fault zone – Son, Narmada, Tapti – runs east–west across the shield’s waist, reactivated in age after age; it explains the two west-flowing rivers, much of where the Traps’ feeders rose, and the plateau’s northern edge.

    Process · The moving plate

  • c. 1,100 Ma The kimberlites of Wajrakarur

    Pipes of deep-mantle rock that shot through the craton about 1,100 million years ago, carrying diamonds; in the current reconstruction, the likeliest primary source of Golconda’s stones.

    Formation · The old craton

  • c. 300–250 Ma Talchir ice and Gondwana coal

    An ice age’s boulder beds, then river-swamp forests of Glossopteris, filled the Godavari rift – the coal that fires the modern Deccan was laid down when the plateau stood near the South Pole.

    Formation · Sedimentary basins

03

The Breakup

c. 250–67 Ma

Gondwana comes apart, the coasts are made, and India casts off for its sprint north with dinosaurs aboard.

  • c. 180–88 Ma The breakup of Gondwana

    The supercontinent tore apart in stages, and each tearing made a piece of India’s outline; the parting from Madagascar, about 88 million years ago, drew the west coast the Ghats now stand behind.

    Process · The moving plate

  • c. 70–66 Ma Dinosaurs at the edge of the Traps

    The Lameta beds along the plateau’s northern rim preserve India’s last dinosaurs – titanosaurs and the predator Rajasaurus, with nesting grounds of fossil eggs – sealed beneath the first lava flows.

    Formation · Life

04

The Traps

c. 67–65 Ma

The event itself: a million cubic kilometres of basalt in less than a million years, the step landscape of the western Deccan, and a share – still argued over – in the death of the dinosaurs.

  • c. 66.3–65.5 Ma The Deccan Traps

    Flood basalts a kilometre and more thick buried the western peninsula in geologically instantaneous floods of lava – the event the collection is named for, and the rock the western Deccan is made of.

    Formation · The Traps

  • c. 66 Ma Between the flows

    In the pauses between eruptions, lakes and soils formed on the cooling lava; their thin fossil-rich layers – the intertrappean beds – are the Deccan’s diary of the extinction interval.

    Formation · Life

  • 66.0 Ma The Traps and the death of the dinosaurs

    The eruptions bracket the extinction that ended the Mesozoic; whether the Deccan shared the killing with the Chicxulub asteroid is one of science’s longest-running trials.

    Event · Life

  • c. 66 Ma – present The Réunion plume

    In the standard model, the mantle upwelling that fed the Traps did not die with them: as India moved on, the plume’s later work built the Laccadives, the Maldives and Mauritius, and it erupts today at Réunion.

    Process · The Traps

05

The Shaping

c. 65–2.6 Ma

The plateau tilts east, the Great Escarpment retreats from the young coast, the rivers turn their backs on the sea, laterite crusts the flat country, and the monsoon assembles.

  • c. 65 Ma – present The Great Escarpment

    In a leading geomorphological model, the Western Ghats are less a range that rose than an edge that retreats – the uplifted flank of the rifted coast, renewed by later uplift and eaten eastward by the monsoon for sixty million years.

    Process · Landforms and rivers

  • c. 60 Ma – present Rivers that turn their backs on the sea

    The Godavari rises eighty kilometres from the Arabian Sea and flows fourteen hundred to the Bay of Bengal; the plateau’s eastward tilt – inherited from successive rifting, volcanism and uplift – underlies them all.

    Process · Landforms and rivers

  • Cenozoic; named 1807 Laterite

    Rain and time rot rock into a rusty crust that cuts soft and hardens in air; Francis Buchanan named it at Angadipuram in 1807, from the Latin for brick.

    Formation · Laterite

  • Cenozoic; reorganised through the Oligocene and Miocene The monsoon assembled

    As the Himalaya and Tibet rose, the seasonal reversal of winds hardened into the monsoon; the Ghats catch it, the plateau lives on the remainder.

    Process · Landforms and rivers

06

Ice, Soil and the First People

c. 2.6 Ma–3000 BC

The Quaternary Deccan: black soil forms on the basalt, handaxe makers camp at Hunsgi, and the ash of a Sumatran supervolcano falls on Kurnool.

  • perhaps 1.2 Ma – c. 300,000 years ago Handaxes at Hunsgi

    In a limestone-floored valley near the Krishna, generations of early hominins camped, quarried and left thousands of handaxes – the Deccan’s deepest human archive.

    Place · People and stone

  • Quaternary Regur, the black cotton soil

    The Traps weather into deep black clays that crack in the dry and swell in the rain, hold moisture through the year – and grew the cotton on which the Deccan’s modern history turned.

    Formation · Soil

  • c. 74,000 years ago The Toba ash at Jwalapuram

    Ash from the greatest eruption of the last two million years, at Toba in Sumatra, blankets a Deccan valley – with stone tools below the ash and above it, at the centre of a fierce argument.

    Event · Life

07

The Plateau Read and Used

3000 BC–present

The rock enters history: ashmounds and megaliths, wootz steel, cave temples cut into basalt, forts of granite, trap and laterite, diamonds sieved from river gravel – and at last the geologists, reading the whole story back.

  • c. 3000–1200 BC The ashmounds

    The Deccan Neolithic’s strangest monuments: hills of vitrified cattle-dung ash, burned and reburned over centuries by the plateau’s first herding villages.

    Place · People and stone

  • c. 1200 BC – early centuries AD Megaliths and iron

    The Iron Age Deccan buried its dead under granite – dolmens, circles and cists by the thousand, as at Hire Benakal – while its smiths learned the iron that became the region’s craft signature.

    Place · People and stone

  • aquifers since the Traps; wells since antiquity Water between the flows

    Unfractured basalt has very low permeability; the Deccan’s wells live in the pile’s openings – weathered mantles, joints and fractures, vesicular flow tops and the horizons between flow and flow. Where a well works is the lava pile’s anatomy, read from below.

    Process · The Traps · Landforms and rivers

  • c. 200 BC – AD 1000 Caves cut into the Traps

    From Bhaja and Karla to Ajanta and Ellora, a thousand years of monks and patrons carved halls, monasteries and whole temples out of the lava pile – architecture by subtraction, in the Deccan’s own rock.

    Place · The Traps · People and stone

  • c. AD 1100–1700 The stones of the forts

    Every fortress in the Deccan timeline is a geology lesson: trap scarps at Daulatabad and the Maratha strongholds, granite at Golconda and Hampi, laterite at Bidar – the dynasties built with whatever period of this collection they stood on.

    Place · People and stone

  • washed to c. AD 1700 The diamond gravels of the Krishna

    The river finished what the kimberlites began: at Kollur and the Krishna washings, Golconda’s diamonds were sieved from gravels the craton had been shedding for a billion years.

    Place · Sedimentary basins · People and stone

  • origins disputed; documented 16th–19th c. Wootz

    In sealed crucibles, Deccan smiths made the watered crucible steel of the Damascus blades – a Telangana industry securely documented from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century, with claimed origins far older, that Europe admired, bought and struggled to reproduce reliably.

    Object · People and stone

  • 1819–1900 Reading the rock

    Company surgeons and surveyors – Voysey, Sykes, Newbold – began the Deccan’s geology; the Geological Survey of India, founded 1851, and the Blanford generation turned it into a history.

    Document · People and stone

Codas

  • 1980 – present Coda: the argument about the Traps

    Impact against volcano, plume against plate: since 1980 the Deccan has been a proving ground for how the earth sciences argue – and the plateau’s deep history is still being revised beneath it.

    Document · The Traps

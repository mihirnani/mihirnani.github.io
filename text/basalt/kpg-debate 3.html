The Traps and the death of the dinosaurs

66.0 Ma · Event · Anjar, Kutch, Gujarat

The eruptions bracket the extinction that ended the Mesozoic; whether the Deccan shared the killing with the Chicxulub asteroid is one of science's longest-running trials.

Two catastrophes coincide at the Cretaceous–Palaeogene boundary: the Chicxulub impact in Mexico, and the main pulse of the Deccan eruptions. Since the impact hypothesis of 1980, and Courtillot's counter-case for the Traps soon after, the extinction has been argued like a trial with two defendants. The volcanic case rests on scale and timing – gases from a million cubic kilometres of lava, with the largest flows erupting within a geological instant of the boundary; the impact case rests on the global iridium layer and the crater itself. The current mainstream verdict makes Chicxulub the killing stroke, with the Traps stressing the world's climate before and after; a persistent minority, associated above all with Gerta Keller's group – whose evidence includes sections in the Deccan's own intertrappean and Kutch localities such as Anjar – gives the volcanism the larger role. Precision dating has narrowed the questions without yet closing them.

India holds unusual evidence either way. The boundary falls inside the Trap pile, so the K–Pg interval lies within the eruptive sequence, and intertrappean sections preserve dinosaur remains and proposed impact markers – at Anjar, iridium-bearing layers interleaved with basalts – whose exact stratigraphic relations remain debated. And in 2015 the impact and the plume were joined in a further hypothesis: that seismic energy from Chicxulub accelerated the Deccan's eruptions, making the two defendants accomplices. The case continues.

However the shares are finally assigned, the plateau's place in the story is settled: the Deccan is one of the two named suspects in the most famous death in natural history, and the only one you can stand on.

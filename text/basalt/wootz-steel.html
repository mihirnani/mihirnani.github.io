Wootz

origins disputed; documented 16th–19th c. · Object · Konasamudram, Telangana

In sealed crucibles, Deccan smiths made the watered crucible steel of the Damascus blades – a Telangana industry securely documented from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century, with claimed origins far older, that Europe admired, bought and struggled to reproduce reliably.

At some disputed date – claims reach back toward the Iron Age, but the secure archaeology is much later – peninsular smiths perfected crucible steel: iron sealed in clay crucibles and fired for hours until it took up carbon and melted, cast as small ingots or ‘cakes’. The Deccani process was not one recipe: alongside the carburising of wrought iron with plant matter, the smiths of these districts co-fused bloomery iron with cast iron, and the craft is reconstructed largely from crucibles, ingots and blades rather than texts. Forged with skill, the ingots yielded blades with the flowing, watered pattern that trade named Damascus, after the market rather than the maker; the English word for the material, wootz, is an eighteenth-century garbling of a south Indian term. The Deccan – with Telangana's Konasamudram and the districts around it the most famous centres – was the industry's documented heartland from at least the sixteenth century to the nineteenth, its ingots exported west through the ports of the other collections' entries.

The geology beneath the craft was specific: iron ores of the right purity, granulitic and greenstone-belt sources for flux and crucible clays, and forest enough for charcoal – a combination the plateau's basement supplied and the smiths' empirical chemistry exploited. Modern metallurgy has explained the pattern (carbide banding, with trace elements from particular ores implicated in the best blades) largely by studying surviving ingots and blades; European attempts to reproduce the material, from the Royal Society's examination of wootz in the 1790s to Faraday's experiments, helped seed modern alloy steel research even as the Indian industry itself declined under imported metal in the nineteenth century.

Wootz is the plateau's metallurgical signature: an Archaean endowment, a perfected furnace craft and a global market meeting in village furnaces – the Deccan exporting, for once, not stones or crops but a secret.

In the story

Between the megaliths' iron and the Survey's mineralogy stands wootz: the craft that made the Deccan's basement famous on three continents. It pairs with the diamond entries – two exports of the deep rock, one carried by rivers, one by furnaces.

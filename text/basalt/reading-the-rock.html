Reading the rock

1819–1900 · Document · Hyderabad, Telangana

Company surgeons and surveyors – Voysey, Sykes, Newbold – began the Deccan’s geology; the Geological Survey of India, founded 1851, and the Blanford generation turned it into a history.

The Deccan’s rocks acquired their readers in the same decades, and often in the same persons, as the map collection’s surveyors. Henry Voysey, surgeon-geologist to the Great Trigonometrical Survey under Lambton, described the granite of Hyderabad and the diamond country in the 1820s – the first systematic geology of the plateau, done alongside the triangulation of the other collection. W. H. Sykes, statistician of the Deccan in the Bombay entries’ era, catalogued the ‘trap’ country’s rocks and fossils; T. J. Newbold, a Madras officer, worked across the peninsula in the 1840s with a special eye for the laterite, the Kurnool caves and the diamond tracts. What they founded piecemeal, the state then organised: the Geological Survey of India, established in 1851 – older than the Crown Raj it served – mapped the peninsula sheet by sheet, and in 1879 produced the first connected account, H. B. Medlicott and W. T. Blanford’s Manual of the Geology of India, in which the Deccan Traps, the Gondwana system and laterite all appear under the names this collection still uses. The Survey’s most relentless field man in the south was Robert Bruce Foote, who mapped the Madras Presidency for thirty-three years, named the Dharwar System in 1888 and founded the Mysore Geological Department in 1894; he is remembered as much for what he lifted out of a lateritic gravel at Pallavaram in May 1863 – the first stone tool in India recognised as palaeolithic – as for the rocks he mapped.

Their reading had its motives – coal for the railways of the Deccan timeline’s period seven, iron, gold assessments at Kolar, building stone – and its blind spots, and like the map-makers they depended on unnamed Indian labour and knowledge: quarrymen, well-diggers and mine-workers who knew the rocks’ behaviour long before their stratigraphy. But the intellectual event was real. Between Voysey’s notebooks and the Manual, the plateau stopped being a surface with resources and became a history – eruptions, submergences, ice ages, extinctions – stretching behind the human record that the site’s other collections narrate.

That is where this collection hands over. The geologists’ Deccan – deep, layered, datable – is the one these entries have retold; the ground the Company was surveying for revenue in the other timeline was, in the same offices, acquiring a past four billion years long.

In the story

The collection ends like the map collection: with the observers. The same Company decades that produced Lambton’s triangles and Elphinstone’s Report produced the Deccan’s first geological readers – and the deep history this collection narrates is, itself, an artefact of the era its companion timeline describes.

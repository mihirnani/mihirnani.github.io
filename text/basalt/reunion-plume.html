The Réunion plume

c. 66 Ma – present · Process · Réunion, Indian Ocean

In the standard model, the mantle upwelling that fed the Traps did not die with them: as India moved on, the plume’s later work built the Laccadives, the Maldives and Mauritius, and it erupts today at Réunion.

The standard account of the Traps ties them to a mantle plume – a column of unusually hot rock rising from deep in the mantle – whose massive head reached the base of the Indian plate about 66 million years ago and melted on a scale continents rarely see. The plate, moving north at its Cretaceous sprint, then slid off the plume; the plume stayed. Its subsequent output, tapped through whatever crust happened to be passing, is written across the floor of the Indian Ocean as a chain: the Laccadive and Chagos–Maldive ridges, the Mascarene plateau, Mauritius, and finally the island of Réunion, where the volcano Piton de la Fournaise erupts in the present tense. Read backwards, the chain is a tape recording of India’s motion; read forwards, it is the Traps’ long retirement.

The plume model has always had dissenters – there are geologists who derive flood basalts from plate-boundary processes and shallow mantle instead – and the argument is one of the standing debates of the science. But the geometry is hard to argue with: the oldest rocks of the chain sit beside the youngest Traps, and the ages young steadily southward for 5,000 kilometres. Few places on earth let you follow a single volcanic engine across sixty-six million years and an ocean.

For the Deccan the plume is also a reminder of scale: the event that made the plateau’s western half was one incident in the career of a structure that outlived it by the whole length of this collection’s remaining periods, and is not finished.

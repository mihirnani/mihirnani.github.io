About the Collection

About

The making of the Deccan: the plateau's deep history, from the Archaean craton to the first geologists, told as a collection of entries in the manner of this site's historical timelines.

This site's other collections treat the Deccan as a stage: maps drawn of it, states built on it. This one treats the stage as the story. It runs from the grey gneisses of the southern craton, through the covering basins and the breakup of Gondwana, to the Trap eruptions, the shaping of escarpment, river, laterite and black soil, and the first peoples and industries of the rock – ending, like the map collection, with the observers: the Company surgeons and the Geological Survey who first read the plateau's deep history in the nineteenth century.

What it is not

It is not a geology textbook, and it does not pretend to advance the science. It is a reader's retelling of the standard account, arranged so that the deep history and the human history of the same ground can be read against each other; the entries lean deliberately toward what the rock later meant – forts, soils, diamonds, coal, steel – as much as how it formed.

How it is arranged

Seven periods, from the Basement to the Plateau Read and Used, with a coda inside the living scientific argument about the Traps. The band on the front page gives each period an equal panel, with positions inside a panel on a logarithmic scale: deep time does not fit a ruler, and the collection does not pretend otherwise. Entries carry a material and a kind as filters, a place on the sketch map, and – where the ground surfaces in the human record – links into the companion Deccan timeline.

On dates and certainty

Geological dates are measurements with uncertainties, and several of this collection's central numbers – the duration of the Trap pulses, the age of the monsoon, the antiquity of the Hunsgi camps, the meaning of the Toba ash – are actively argued in the literature. Following this site's practice, the entries state the mainstream reading, name the disputes, and mark estimates as such. Expect some of these pages to be revised as the science moves; the coda is about exactly that.

Sources

Each entry cites the scholarship it rests on – Valdiya and Krishnan for the regional framework, the monographic and journal literature for particular problems, and the nineteenth-century primary texts, Buchanan to the Survey's Manual, where they are themselves part of the story – with encyclopaedic links set apart as quick reference. The collection is open and non-commercial, assembled for study.

About the Collection

About

A collection of entries on the Deccan across five centuries, from the founding of Vijayanagara and the Bahmani sultanate to the Deccan Riots of 1875, arranged as a timeline in seven periods – and an argument about how sovereignty there was shared, and then was not.

Between the 1330s and the 1870s the plateau south of the Narmada was ruled by more powers at once than any other part of India: the Bahmani sultanate and then its five successors, Vijayanagara and its nayakas, the Mughal empire, the Marathas under a king and then under the Peshwas, the Nizam at Hyderabad, Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan at Mysore, the Portuguese at Goa and the East India Company at Madras and Bombay. They fought, but they also taxed the same villages, hired the same soldiers, recognised one another’s grants and sent embassies to one another’s courts. This collection reads the period as the story of that layered sovereignty: made in the fourteenth century, reordered between 1799 and 1831 into a hierarchy under a single paramount power, and then, between 1831 and 1875, made legible and subordinate – when the hereditary claims to revenue, office and service the Company had inherited were measured, called in and adjudicated, and finally entered in a register under the Bombay Hereditary Offices Act. The colonial state did not abolish the old order; it audited it. The hereditary offices themselves survived, as statutory categories, until independent India abolished them between 1950 and 1963. The entries separate the documentary record (the body of each page) from the collection’s own reading of it (the paragraph headed “In the story”); where the body ventures an interpretation it says so.

How it is arranged

Six periods, broadly chronological: Two Foundations (c. 1330–1500), when Delhi withdrew from the Deccan and Vijayanagara and the Bahmani sultanate rose amid the Reddi, Velama and other regional states; Two Deccans (1500–1565); The Mughal South (1565–1687); The Maratha Century (1687–1761); Mysore and the Company Wars (1761–1799); Paramountcy (1799–1831); and The Settlement (1831–1875), which closes the argument. Two entries are marked as codas and sit outside the numbered chronology: Ranade’s generation of historians, and the Company’s maps. Within each, the entries are of mixed kinds – battles and treaties, but also people, places, documents, objects and institutions – because a treaty or a revenue grant usually explains more than a battle does. The timeline on the front page places every entry on the row of its principal polity, and the filters let the collection be read by polity or by kind. The core chronology runs from about 1336 to 1875; a few contextual entries begin in the late fifteenth century, and a cartographic coda follows the story into the later nineteenth century.

How the entries were written

Each entry was checked against the standard scholarship: Richard Eaton on the Deccan and on Raichur, Haroon Khan Sherwani on the Bahmanis and Mahmud Gawan, Peter Jackson on the Delhi sultanate, Burton Stein and Noboru Karashima on Vijayanagara, Gajanan Bhaskar Mehendale on Shivaji, Uday S. Kulkarni on Bajirao, Nanasaheb and Panipat, Stewart Gordon and André Wink on the Marathas, Kate Brittlebank on Tipu, John Richards on the Mughals, Ravinder Kumar, Neil Charlesworth and Sumit Guha on the Bombay Deccan under the Company, and the Cambridge histories. Dates that sources dispute are given as disputed. Popular framings are avoided in both directions: Talikota is not treated as the end of a civilisation, Tipu is neither tyrant nor martyr, and the Marathas are a state-building project rather than a nation in waiting. Each page lists its sources, and readers who find an error are asked to say so.

The companion collection

The same site holds The European Gaze on India, a collection of European maps of India from 1519 to 1946. Where a map illuminates an entry – d’Anville’s 1752 India for the Carnatic wars, Rennell for the Company’s first view of the Deccan, Lambton’s triangulation, the Vandermaelen sheets of Bijapur and Aurangabad – the entry links to it, and the coda to the fifth period hands the reader over to the maps, where the Deccan appears as the Company drew it.

Terms

The collection is open and non-commercial, assembled for study. Place names follow modern scholarly usage with the older form noted where it matters: Talikota (Rakkasa-Tangadi), Srirangapatna (Seringapatam), Bijapur (Vijayapura), Golconda, Hampi (Vijayanagara).
